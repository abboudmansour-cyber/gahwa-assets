The Lead
​
Two tankers carrying Saudi oil were reportedly struck in the Strait of Hormuz, and the kingdom went back to the debt market the same morning.
The United States launched airstrikes on Iran targeting Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps air defence, radar, minelaying and communications capabilities in response to what Washington called attempted attacks on commercial shipping.

Iran's parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf warned that US attacks could lead to a blockade on all oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz, according to his statement. The US Treasury Secretary described Iran as in a 'death spiral' after saying, per his account, Tehran reportedly attempted to rebuild radar installations along the waterway before the strikes. Iran said the US strikes killed 11 people, including four at a wedding in southern Sirik county.

Brent crude traded at $95.53 a barrel on the day, up 0.9%, and global bond yields hit multi-decade highs in Japan, the UK, Germany, France and the US as the renewed fighting reportedly fuelled inflation fears. The US 10-year Treasury yield edged close to 4.8%, a level not seen since January 2025. The attack is, according to Gulf operators, the latest in a series of incidents around the waterway that have pushed carriers to re-route, lift war-risk surcharges and delay expansion plans from Dubai to Muscat. One tanker, Burgan, reported a security incident while transiting the strait, and another was struck by three unknown projectiles 17 nautical miles east of Khasab, Oman, per JMIC. The strait carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil, so the convoy of decisions trailing every fireball is what Gulf businesses now budget around. The strait's status is now the single largest variable in every Gulf freight, fuel and insurance cost line.

02Saudi fiscal strain
Saudi returns to debt market despite May pause
Saudi Arabia issued $3.25 billion in two benchmark-sized USD sukuk tranches at five and ten years, months after the National Debt Management Center said it had largely finished borrowing for the year. The order book exceeded $16.5 billion, and pricing tightened from initial thoughts of about 100 basis points over US Treasuries on the five-year tranche to a final spread of 70 basis points, with the ten-year landing at 80 basis points.

The kingdom ran a SAR 125.7 billion deficit in the first quarter of 2026, more than double the first quarter of 2025 and already most of the SAR 165.4 billion projected for the full year. Aramco raised USD 4 billion in February, and the Public Investment Fund secured USD 7 billion in May. The National Debt Management Center and Aramco are now in separate negotiations for new loans from international banks.

The return to the market signals that pre-war financing assumptions no longer hold, and Gulf issuers are competing with a global treasury sell-off that has pushed yields to multi-decade highs. For Gulf corporates, the read is simple: the sovereign is back as a borrower at the same moment the market is in no mood to offer easy pricing. The result is a tighter, more expensive liquidity backdrop for everyone else raising money this quarter. When the kingdom re-enters the market early, it tells every Gulf issuer that capital is about to get scarcer.

03Tokenised equities
Coinbase puts Apple, Meta tokens on Base
Coinbase launched four tokenized equity tokens, NVDAc, METAc, AAPLc and GOOGLc, on its Base blockchain for non-US users under a new B20 standard. The launch generated $10.8 million in 24-hour volume and $3.06 million in decentralised exchange liquidity across about 50 protocols.

The tokenized public equities sector now reaches $2.48 billion in total, with Ondo leading at $872.7 million. ICE, the parent of the New York Stock Exchange, partnered with tZERO on tokenized securities infrastructure, and ICE will invest in tZERO's latest funding round while licensing its 103-patent blockchain portfolio.

The pairing of a global exchange group with a token platform is a signal that the infrastructure for round-the-clock equity trading is being built in public, with Gulf markets next in line for the same treatment as liquidity moves to chains. Once equities trade as tokens around the clock, Gulf exchanges will face a version of the same pressure to keep up.

04Restaurant software
Foodics Black targets full-service restaurants
Foodics unveiled Foodics Black, an all-in-one restaurant management platform for dine-in full-service restaurants, at LEAP in Riyadh. The platform is built on Foodics' cloud infrastructure and connects operations across the entire guest journey, from reservation through payment.

The launch targets fine dining and contemporary casual concepts, a segment where legacy point-of-sale systems still dominate and where table management and kitchen routing are more complex than in quick service. Foodics is a Saudi-founded company, and the product arrives as the kingdom's restaurant market continues to grow on the back of the entertainment and events calendar.

For Gulf operators, the pitch is a single system that handles covers, tabs and service flow without stitching together three vendors. The timing is sharp: full-service restaurants in Riyadh and Dubai are now competing as much on table turn and order accuracy as on menu, and software that shortens the gap between kitchen and card machine is worth more than another loyalty feature. Full-service restaurants have spent years buying four systems when one now covers the whole floor.

05AI distribution
Salam, UnifyApps push AI in Saudi
Etihad Salam Telecom Company and UnifyApps inked an AI-adoption deal at LEAP 2026, and the memorandum of understanding will help government entities and enterprise customers across Saudi Arabia adopt AI and become AI-native organisations. Salam will act as the single point of contact for sales, support and delivery.

The partnership brings together UnifyApps' AI platform with Salam's regulatory and operational reach inside the kingdom, at a moment when Saudi firms have committed more than $2 billion to new compute capacity at the same event. The AI market in the kingdom is shifting from pilot projects to deployment, and local partners who can navigate data sovereignty and licensing are becoming the gatekeepers of that spend.

For Saudi operators, the signal is that AI adoption is being channelled through licensed local partners rather than sold direct, which means the partner network, not the frontier model, will decide how fast government demand converts into contracts. Saudi AI spending is moving from runway experiments to local partnerships, and the companies that sign those agreements now will own the deployment market.

The AI Race
​
OpenAI's Astra can break into systems
OpenAI previewed precautions ahead of releasing Astra, its newest large language model, which the company says is very good at breaking into computer systems. The model will be shown to select partners first for cyber defence preparation. This is the first time OpenAI has flagged an AI model with critical cyber capabilities before launch. The move follows a broader industry push, with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and 100 other companies signing a joint cyber defence letter.

The letter came after a Redwood and METR report described AI agents coordinating on their own. The shift is from wondering whether models can probe infrastructure to deciding who gets the keys first. A model that defends systems is the same model that can test them, so every release doubles as a security tool. OpenAI is not hiding the capability, it is naming it before regulators do. Gulf chief security officers should treat the label as an urgent procurement question, not a headline.

Taqa exits ADX after delisting completes
Abu Dhabi National Energy Company left the Abu Dhabi exchange after ADPower, owned by L'imad, lifted its stake to 98.12% and moved to buy out remaining minority shareholders. The delisting closes the final chapter of a takeover that reshapes the emirate's utilities ownership. The move takes one of Abu Dhabi's largest listed utilities off the public market.
ADIB plans AED 1.75bn rights issue
Abu Dhabi Islamic Bank signed off on a proposal to raise AED 1.75 billion through a rights issue, subject to shareholder and central bank approval. The offer is priced at a 28.8% discount to the last closing price before the announcement. The bank is raising capital at a moment when Gulf issuers face tighter liquidity.
Mubadala's du sell-down lifts freefloat
Mubadala raised AED 3.15 billion by selling shares in du, lifting the telecom operator's freefloat to 27.7% and paving the way for MSCI inclusion. It was the UAE's first fully marketed secondary share offer. The sale puts more of du in public hands just as index inclusion demand rises.
Volkswagen plans four German plant closures
Volkswagen plans to end production at four German plants between 2031 and 2034, with Emden and Zwickau closing in 2031, Hanover in 2032, and Neckarsulm in 2034. The group's operating result fell 11.6% to $6.9 billion in the first half of 2026. Its operating margin dropped to 3.8% from 4.2% a year earlier.

The Last Sip

Which Gulf city has more registered cats than cars?
The answer
Kuwait City is often cited for its enormous stray cat population, estimated in the hundreds of thousands across the country. Animal welfare groups run feeding programs and trap-neuter-release efforts year round. The city has tried several campaigns to manage the colonies, with limited success. The cats have simply out-organized the municipality. The only residents who do not care about the traffic.
What was the longest road in the world built by an airline?
The answer
Oman Air's road operation between Dubai and Muscat was built not as a road, but as a cargo route using trucks instead of a freighter aircraft. It ran more than 120 round trips and carried over 1.5 million kilograms of cargo since May. The airline plans to extend the service to Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar and Saudi Arabia. When you sell your only freighter, the highway is the next best thing.
