The Lead
​
Iran is now shopping a single shipping lane through the Strait of Hormuz with Oman's signature, while the US Navy is busy clearing mines from the waterway it has redirected 82 ships around.
Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian got supreme leader sign-off on a Hormuz route Tehran and Oman have agreed on, the narrow waterway that carries a fifth of the world's oil. The route has been communicated to Iran's supreme leader, signaling the highest-level sign-off on the proposal.

The offer comes with a condition: Iran will reopen the strait only if the United States fulfills commitments under the Islamabad Memorandum signed in June. Those commitments include lifting sanctions and the maritime blockade, releasing frozen assets, beginning investment, and ending the war.

US naval forces have already redirected 82 commercial vessels, disabled 3, and boarded 2 as of August 28, while clearing sea mines from the waterway. Admiral Brad Cooper said the military has helped about 1,500 vessels carrying nearly 750 million barrels of crude oil cross through since the conflict began.

For Gulf exporters, a single agreed transit route would be a step toward normalizing shipping flows, but the political conditions attached mean no relief is imminent. Qatari LNG shipments to Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Italy remain under force majeure. Iran has authorized vessels to use a temporary passage through the middle of the strait, but Iranian officials have warned of psychological warfare aimed at making the blockade seem ineffective. The gap between a diplomatic offer and an operational shipping lane remains wide enough to keep insurance premiums and rerouting costs elevated for Gulf operators, analysts say, per sources familiar with the talks. For now, the offer is a signal of diplomatic flexibility, not a guarantee of open water.

02Leadership shakeup
New Murabba replaces chief executive
Saudi Arabia's $50 billion New Murabba giga-project has replaced its chief executive, with PIF senior executive Sabah Barakat stepping in as acting CEO. Barakat replaces Michael Dyke, who had led the Riyadh redevelopment since January 2024.

Barakat also serves as acting group CEO of Roshn Group, meaning he now oversees two of the Kingdom's largest real estate ventures simultaneously. The 14-square-kilometer project is designed for 400,000 residents across 18 communities, but its completion date has already slipped to 2040, a decade later than originally planned.

The leadership change follows PIF's order for minimum spending cuts of 20 percent across its portfolio in 2025, affecting more than 100 companies. Work on the project's centerpiece, the 400-meter-high cube-shaped Mukaab tower, was suspended per a January Reuters report. Saudi Arabia began accepting applications from non-Saudis to purchase property in New Murabba in June, a shift that makes the project's execution timeline increasingly relevant to foreign investors. The executive replacement at one of Riyadh's most visible giga-projects reflects the broader pressure on PIF to consolidate leadership and control costs across its sprawling real estate portfolio. The move signals that PIF is prioritizing operational discipline over expansion in its real estate ventures.

03Fed policy
Warsh keeps rate hikes open
Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole speech to signal that interest rate hikes remain possible if inflation does not fall. The PCE price index rose 3.7 percent over the past year and 4.1 percent over the past six months.

Warsh outlined a shift to limit forward guidance on future interest-rate decisions, effectively keeping the Fed's options open in both directions. Three policymakers voted to hike rates at last month's meeting, and Warsh appears to be edging toward that view. Short-term US government bond yields rose as traders reportedly increased their bets on a September hike.

For Gulf economies with currencies pegged to the dollar, the Fed's path determines domestic interest rates. A rate hike would tighten liquidity in the GCC at the same time that governments are funding large-scale investment programs. The persistence of US inflation at 3.7 percent means Gulf central banks may need to follow any Fed move to defend their dollar pegs. A September Fed hike would ripple through Saudi and UAE interbank rates within days of the decision.

04Desalination contract
Wabag wins $372m Kuwait desalination deal
India's Wabag has confirmed a $372 million contract to build a seawater reverse osmosis desalination facility in Kuwait with capacity of 60 million imperial gallons a day. The project addresses Kuwait's growing water demand, which has been strained by wartime pressures.

The contract is one of several infrastructure awards moving forward in the Gulf despite the regional conflict. Kuwait's Mina Abdullah and Mina Al-Ahmadi refineries, hit several times since the war began, are also undergoing repairs by Samsung E&A following Iranian attacks, according to reports.

For Gulf water and energy operators, the award signals that infrastructure spending continues even amid conflict. The desalination plant will add significant capacity to Kuwait's water grid, reducing the country's vulnerability to supply disruptions in the Arabian Gulf. Wabag's selection also underscores the continued role of Indian engineering firms in Gulf infrastructure projects. The contract is a concrete step in Kuwait's push for water security in a conflict-prone region.

Quantum crypto
Ripple preps XRP Ledger for quantum
Ripple is preparing the XRP Ledger for quantum computers before 'Q-Day' arrives, following an Anthropic model that cut the work needed to break a leading post-quantum signature candidate by a factor of 67 million last month. Bitcoin and Ethereum published their own quantum migration plans this week.

The quantum threat to cryptography is no longer theoretical, as the 67-million-fold reduction in attack complexity reportedly demonstrates. The Anthropic breakthrough accelerated the timeline for blockchain networks to address the risk that quantum computers could eventually break the cryptographic signatures securing transactions.

For Gulf fintech and crypto firms, the quantum migration trend has direct implications. Payment networks and digital asset platforms built on older cryptographic standards face the same exposure. Ripple's proactive approach, along with Bitcoin and Ethereum's published plans, signals that the industry is moving from research to implementation. The Gulf's emerging crypto hubs will need to match this pace to keep investor confidence. The quantum clock is ticking for every crypto platform that has not published a migration plan.

The AI Race
​
Lambda borrows its way into the chip business
Neocloud Lambda has raised $1 billion in private debt to buy Nvidia AI chips that will be leased to Microsoft. The deal is part of a string of loans underwriting the high cost of the AI boom. Lambda's model is to own the chips and rent them to hyperscalers, earning the gap between debt service and lease revenue.

The bet works as long as Microsoft and its peers keep signing leases for AI compute. Nvidia's quarterly revenue hit $96.2 billion, up 106 percent year-over-year, but its free cash flow halved to $21 billion. The risk is that chip prices compress as Nvidia ramps supply, squeezing the economics of leveraged chip purchases. For the AI race, this is a sign that infrastructure is becoming a debt market as much as a technology race. Sovereign wealth funds are evaluating similar structures, which means Gulf data center plans now compete with debt terms, not just chip access.
