Reportedly, Riyadh absorbs strikes
Saudi Arabia is moving its flagship Future Investment Initiative conference to a hybrid format after Houthi missile attacks closed King Khalid International Airport, officials said. The gathering is still set for October 26-29, but delegates will now be able to attend remotely. The shift is the clearest sign yet that the Kingdom's business calendar is adapting to a war that has made its capital a target. The attacks on Riyadh have killed at least 13 people and injured hundreds, according to Saudi state media. The airport closure has forced airlines to cancel flights to the capital until at least Saturday. Informa partners with the Kingdom through its Tahaluf joint venture. The events company has already postponed 15 events worth $175 million in revenue since the Iran war began. CEO Stephen Carter said the company is now planning for delegates to fly into airstrips outside Riyadh or other Gulf states and drive in.

Why the conference matters FII is the Kingdom's most important business gathering, a Davos for the Gulf that draws global finance and tech leaders. Keeping it on the calendar is a signal to investors that Saudi Arabia remains open for business. Reportedly, the hybrid format is also a practical solution to the immediate problem of getting people into a city under sporadic missile attack. The bigger question is how many will want to come in person at all.

The economic context is tightening. Moody's now forecasts PIF spending of SAR 600-800 billion over 2026-2030, down from around SAR 750 billion in the prior five-year cycle. The rating agency expects the government to reprioritize toward projects that generate cash sooner. The war is accelerating a shift already underway in how the Kingdom allocates capital. For a Kingdom selling itself as a stable investment destination, the measure of success is no longer how many people show up. It is how many keep coming back.

02Cash-flow Red Sea
Moody's cuts PIF forecast
Moody's sees Saudi Arabia reprioritizing its giga-project portfolio toward developments that generate cash sooner, citing pressure from a war economy and a widening budget deficit. PIF spending is now forecast at SAR 600-800 billion over 2026-2030, down from around SAR 750 billion in the prior five-year cycle. The rating agency sees priority sectors as tourism, housing, mining, manufacturing, logistics, AI, and digital infrastructure. More advanced developments, including some Red Sea assets, are better positioned to generate cashflow.

Earlier-stage projects such as Jeddah Central are more dependent on financing and execution. The Kingdom has already raised its projected budget deficit to SAR 245 billion, or 4.9% of GDP, from SAR 165 billion previously. Moody's cut its oil production forecast for 2026 to 7.7 million barrels per day from 8.4 million.

The shift is a quiet admission that the era of unlimited ambition is over. Saudi Arabia is not cancelling its giga-projects, but it is applying a discount to the ones that cannot pay for themselves soon. That is the difference between building a city and running a country. The projects that survive this cycle will be the ones that can show a return, not just a rendering.

03East coast boom
Khorfakkan throughput jumps 700%
Gulftainer's Khorfakkan terminal saw throughput jump from 8,000 TEUs to 65,000 TEUs weekly. That 700% increase makes it the UAE's main gateway outside the Strait of Hormuz. The port is now set for a $2 billion expansion that will double capacity to 10 million TEUs. The shift is the most visible sign yet that the war has redrawn the region's logistics map. Cargo that once moved through Jebel Ali is now finding its way to the eastern coast.

Gulftainer launched the UAE's largest bonded dry port in Sajaa in 2025, 90 kilometers from Khorfakkan. It added the Al Dhaid Multi-Modal Logistics Hub, a 150-hectare site with annual capacity of 1.5 million TEUs. The strategy is to create an inland network that can feed the port without relying on the Strait. The Adcop pipeline already carries 1.8 million barrels per day to Fujairah.

The east coast is no longer a backup. It is becoming the main event, and the businesses that get there first will be the ones that define the next decade of Gulf logistics. Reportedly, ports that once competed for transshipment scraps are now the safest route to market, and their value is rising with every missile alert.

04No wallet required
Standard Chartered launches crypto trading
Standard Chartered made the UAE its first market where individual clients can buy and sell bitcoin and ether directly through its mobile app. Customers use their existing bank accounts without needing separate crypto wallets. The move is a significant step in the convergence of traditional finance and digital assets in the Gulf.

The bank is betting that UAE regulators have created a clear enough framework to support mainstream crypto adoption. It is also betting that its clients want exposure without the friction of a separate exchange. That is a very different proposition from the early days of crypto as a niche, slightly rebellious asset class.

The UAE's regulators have moved faster than most to write rules for digital assets, and banks are now following. What was once a gray market is becoming a standard product. A bank offering bitcoin is the clearest sign yet that crypto has left the gray market and entered the lobby.

05DAE taps Asia
DAE seeks $2.1 billion loan
Dubai Aerospace Enterprise is seeking a $2.1 billion seven-year loan, mostly from Asian banks. Emirates NBD, Bank of China, OCBC, and China Construction Bank Asia are arranging the facility. The USD tranche could price at 110 basis points over SOFR, with the AED tranche at 95 basis points over EIBOR.

The financing comes less than three months after DAE closed its $9 billion acquisition of Macquarie AirFinance, making it the world's third-largest aircraft lessor. The loan is a vote of confidence from Asian lenders in the Gulf's aviation sector.

The message is clear: even as the region's airports face disruptions, the long-term bet on aviation remains. DAE held $4.4 billion in available liquidity at the end of June, so this is about growth, not survival. Asian banks are lending into the Gulf's aviation story, and that tells you more about the region's prospects than any headline.

The AI Race
​
Nvidia says Gulf AI plans hold
Nvidia says Gulf countries have not abandoned their AI ambitions despite the conflict with Iran. Ruth Berry, who leads global policy on Nvidia's government affairs team, said the first shipments of the company's CPUs and GPUs arrived in the UAE in May. Washington approved the exports after the UAE provided security assurances intended to prevent the chips from reaching unauthorized parties.

Mohammed Soliman, a technology analyst at the Middle East Institute, said the threat of conflict could push the UAE and Saudi Arabia to increase AI investments. His argument is blunt: war is now a function of compute, and the countries that can process information fastest will have the advantage. The Gulf is positioning itself as neutral ground where American and Chinese AI ambitions can both find a home. Gulf AI contracts are now a proxy for the US-China technology war, and the region is happy to play both sides.
