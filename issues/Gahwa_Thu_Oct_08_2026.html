The Lead
​
Saudia's 150-Jet Bet
Saudia Group is lining up what could be its largest aircraft order yet, with at least 150 jets on the table against a current fleet of 196. Deliveries would stretch from 2031 to 2040, according to EVP Sanjiv Kapoor. The carrier has settled on the size but not yet named the manufacturer.

The order signals conviction that Gulf aviation demand survives the current war and rate shock. Saudia is not the only regional carrier betting on post-conflict growth. Riyadh wants a national airline sized to its tourism and connectivity ambitions under Vision 2030.

A fleet addition of this scale needs pilots, maintenance capacity and airport slots, all of which take years to build. Boeing and Airbus will both fight hard for the order, since it is one of the few large wide-body campaigns still open.

Why now matters The commitment to a new fleet arrives while shipping lanes and oil markets stay under war pressure, making aviation one of the few reliable cross-border links left. For Gulf suppliers, it means a long tail of service, training and parts contracts beginning in the early 2030s. The order means a decade of supplier work in training, maintenance and parts will follow the airframe decision, and the competition between Boeing and Airbus will run through the Gulf, not around it.

02Hormuz and Missiles
Houthis Hit Airports
Saudi authorities said three people were killed and 36 wounded in Houthi attacks on Abha and Riyadh's King Khalid international airports, according to officials. The Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed the ballistic missile launch platform used to attack Riyadh, according to the coalition. The Houthis claimed they hit King Khalid International Airport with a ballistic missile and disrupted operations.

One missile reportedly landed near Aden airport's runway minutes before a Cairo flight was due to land, forcing diversion to Jeddah. Saudi-led forces reportedly intercepted another missile north of Riyadh. The attacks come as the US and Iran reportedly hold backchannel talks on terms to end the war, with Washington demanding a meaningful reduction in nuclear enrichment capacity. The escalation keeps pressure on oil markets and on Gulf airspace, where airlines are rerouting flights away from the most exposed corridors.

What is at stake The airport strikes reportedly move the war closer to the Gulf's civilian infrastructure, which changes operating risk for every carrier, insurer and logistics firm in the region. Every airline flying into the Gulf now has to treat airport approaches as a security question, and the insurance and rerouting costs will follow the carriers rather than the country.

03One Cheque Each
Saudi Takes 86% of MENA Funding
Startups across the Middle East and North Africa raised $1.1 billion across 73 deals in September, up 193% from August though still 68% below the same month in 2025. Three deals accounted for about 70% of the monthly total: Barq's $329.5 million Series A, Tabby's $233 million Series F, and Lendo's $200 million debt round.

Saudi Arabia captured $947.2 million across 40 startups, roughly 86% of all MENA funding. Fintech alone pulled in $930.6 million, or 85% of the regional total. The UAE came second with $98.4 million across 12 deals, while Egypt raised $49.2 million.

The pattern is now extreme: Saudi fintech, especially later-stage rounds, is the market. Early-stage deals still drew $418.2 million across 39 transactions, but the headline numbers come from a handful of large cheques.

What the gap says Male-led startups received around $1 billion of the total, while female-founded teams raised only $2.4 million. That imbalance, in a month defined by outliers, is the structural story beneath the rebound. A founder raising outside Saudi fintech this month faced a market where 86% of the capital was already spoken for, so the realistic path is either a Saudi angle or a very patient round.

04Rates and Reserves
Iraq Devalues Dinar
Iraq's central bank devalued the dinar to a selling price of 1,520 to the dollar as the war choked off the Strait of Hormuz and crude exports. Oil is roughly 90% of Iraq's revenue, and its foreign reserves have fallen by about $20 billion. The move makes imports more expensive and deepens pressure on the state budget.

US President Trump said oil flows through Hormuz are back to pre-war levels and claimed Iran's oil minister has resigned. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent questioned the new minister's role, noting tanker-tracking data shows Iran has not loaded a single barrel of crude since late August.

What it signals A devaluation of this scale is a crisis measure, not a realignment. It tells Gulf banks and contractors with Iraqi exposure that payment risk has moved sharply higher, and it pushes the Iraqi government toward emergency borrowing just as global rates sit at two-decade highs. Any Gulf company owed money by an Iraqi state entity should now price the dinar risk explicitly, because the central bank just admitted the old exchange rate could no longer hold.

Leftover gas
Saudi Gas Goes Surplus
Wood Mackenzie estimates up to 50 billion cubic meters a year of Saudi Arabia's 180 billion cubic meter annual gas output could be spare. That would reportedly be enough to make the kingdom a gas exporter for the first time. Aramco's international LNG assets will not be included in the new gas company it is preparing to carve out, according to two people with knowledge of the plans.

The surplus reflects a deliberate expansion of domestic gas production while the kingdom keeps oil output constrained by OPEC policy and war-related logistics. Aramco is now weighing options for the growing surplus, with export terminals and industrial feedstock among the candidates.

What it means here A Saudi gas export capability would reprice regional gas contracts and give Gulf utilities a new supply alternative to Qatari and Iranian flows. It would also strengthen Riyadh's hand in any future negotiation over pipeline routes and pricing. The gas surplus gives Saudi Arabia a new geopolitical lever and Gulf buyers a new bargaining position, but only if the export infrastructure gets approved.

The AI Race
​
The AI Debt Pile Grows
SpaceX is seeking $40 billion in financing to fund Nvidia chip purchases, with $10 billion in bank loans and $30 billion in investment-grade debt. Pimco is among the lenders, and the deal is expected to close in 2027. The company holds an investment-grade credit rating, but investors are demanding substantial extra return on the bonds, a sign of concern over mounting debt and spending plans.

Broadcom is working on a separate debt package of more than $50 billion to finance a custom AI chip with OpenAI, with Apollo and Blackstone approached. Oracle is in talks with Apollo and Goldman Sachs for its own multi-billion AI chip financing. The AI infrastructure buildout is now running on borrowed money, and that changes the risk profile of the whole sector. A Gulf sovereign fund looking at US tech debt will find these are the biggest new opportunities, but also the ones most exposed to a rate shock.
