The Lead · GCC & SAUDI
The Gulf's biggest gas producer just told the Strait of Hormuz it's no longer the only way out.
​
A new east coast LNG plant would let the company load tankers without sending a single cargo through the waterway Iran has kept tightly restricted since February. The disclosure came as Adnoc Gas reported its toughest quarter of the war so far.

Second-quarter net income fell 52% year-on-year to $665 million. Revenue dropped 39% to $3.6 billion. Exports and liquid sales volumes declined 53% as the Hormuz closure reportedly shut in most of the company's seaborne capacity. Brent crude averaged $104 a barrel, 30% higher than the same quarter last year, but bumper oil prices did little for a gas exporter whose ships could not sail.

The company raised its committed 2026, 2030 capex target to $28 billion, up from $20 billion, after reaching a final investment decision on the $6.2 billion Umm Shaif gas cap and pushing ahead with phases two and three of the Rich Gas Development project. CFO Peter van Driel said the east coast study has not yet reached FID. If maritime routes normalise by the fourth quarter, full-year net income is projected at $3.5 to $4 billion. The 2Q dividend held steady at $940 million.
What to do with it
Request a briefing from your logistics lead on east coast port and pipeline capacity; every major Gulf energy exporter is now being pushed to present a credible Hormuz bypass plan to boards and lenders.

02GCC & SAUDI
Aramco's Jazan Refinery Hit Again
A Houthi drone strike on 9 August was, according to MEED, the second attack on the 400,000-barrel-per-day facility in as many weeks. Aramco has delayed the refinery's restart to 30 August while it repairs the gasification complex and tank farm.

The Houthis reportedly claimed responsibility for a weekend strike, saying it was retaliation for Saudi drones breaching Yemeni airspace. Separately, a fire at a sofa factory in Riyadh's Musa Sanaiya industrial area killed 16 Bangladeshi workers on Sunday, per Reuters.

The Jazan refinery sits on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast, well south of the main Gulf theatre, but repeated attacks, reported by officials, show Houthi capability to strike critical energy infrastructure far from their border. The delay pushes back the return of roughly 400,000 barrels a day of refining capacity at a moment when global product markets are already tight. If your supply chain touches Red Sea refined products, build a two-week buffer into August schedules; the Jazan restart is now a live indicator of whether Saudi air defences can hold the southern corridor open.

GCC & SAUDI
Dubai Property Market Cools in Q2
Residential and office activity cooled 19% from a record first quarter, with the latest monthly data pointing to a normalisation rather than a broad correction, according to MEED. After a run that saw Dubai prices post record gains, buyers are turning more selective. The shift is subtle but significant for a market that had been racing at full throttle. Agents report that listings are taking slightly longer to move and price cuts on stale inventory are creeping back, though well-located, quality stock still clears fast. The read-across for Gulf operators is straightforward: the Dubai property cycle is moving from frenzy to discernment, and anyone carrying land or development exposure should be stress-testing their pipeline against a more measured pace of absorption through the second half of the year. If you run pre-construction sales, check your absorption assumptions for Q3 and Q4 against the latest month of transaction data; a selective buyer means your launch timing and pricing precision now matter more than the broader market tailwind.

04MACRO & GEO
Saudi Arabia Finances Deficit Entirely with Debt
The Kingdom financed its entire SAR 160 billion first-half deficit through borrowing, drawing nothing from government reserves, according to EnterpriseAM Saudi. Public debt climbed to SAR 1.685 trillion by the end of June, up from SAR 1.519 trillion at the start of the year.

Financing expenses rose 41% year-on-year in the second quarter alone. Fitch affirmed the country's A+ rating in July and Moody's held its Aa3 in May, with both agencies drawing a line between fiscal stress and geopolitical stress, judging the Kingdom solvent in both scenarios. Risk analyst Rachel Ziemba noted that attracting foreign direct investment into key projects remains difficult, leaving the government as the likely main investor for the foreseeable future. If you hold Saudi government paper or lend into the Kingdom's project pipeline, pull the latest debt maturity schedule from your treasury team; the 41% jump in financing costs in a single quarter is the number your credit committee will want to see stress-tested.

GCC & SAUDI
Mubadala's $3B Brazil Biofuels Bet Grows
Mubadala Capital's Acelen Renováveis is building a $3 billion macauba farming operation across up to 144,000 hectares in Brazil, part of a scaled-up $13.5 billion biofuels ambition spanning five modules. The Bahia biorefinery alone is a $2.5 billion plant, with $1.5 billion in locked-in financing from a 10-bank syndicate led by HSBC and the IFC.

Macauba is the quiet star of the strategy. It produces seven to ten times more oil per hectare than soybeans, and more than 200 researchers are now working on its genetics programme. The refinery is designed to produce 20,000 barrels of sustainable aviation fuel per day once fully operational, just as the EU requires airlines to start cutting emissions in 2027.

The wider market is still tiny: SAF made up 0.6% of all aviation fuel consumed globally last year. Mubadala is effectively building the supply chain before the regulation forces airlines to buy, a classic Mubadala play, own the bottleneck before the rush. If you track SAF mandates or invest in energy transition supply chains, get the Acelen term sheet from your project finance desk; the 7, 10x oil yield per hectare over soy is the number that makes the whole venture look less like a science experiment and more like a commodity play with a regulatory tailwind.
