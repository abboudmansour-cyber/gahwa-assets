The Lead
​
Aramco's Jazan refinery is hit for the third time in six weeks.
The damage is still being assessed after Aramco's Jazan oil facilities were struck yesterday, according to initial reports from the company. This is the third major attack on Jazan in about six weeks, according to local reports. Houthi forces reportedly claimed a drone strike on the refinery on 9 August, after missiles and drones hit the same plant on 27 July, forcing Aramco to shut it down. No refined products shipped from Jazan during August, according to shipping data, after the Houthi attacks resumed. The refinery is a 400,000 barrel per day facility, one of the largest in Saudi Arabia.

Riyadh's draft nuclear pact with Washington shows no binding inspection commitment, a separate development that reframes regional security. The Kingdom will not sign the IAEA's additional protocol, but is negotiating bespoke bilateral provisions to authorize monitoring of uranium enrichment, conversion, and reprocessing. A Wall Street Journal report indicated Riyadh has a path to enrich uranium to nearly 20% under the agreement. The timing puts energy infrastructure and nuclear ambition on the same page of risk.

The market response is already moving. Goldman Sachs expects oil prices to rise as high as $120 a barrel if attacks on shipments intensify, with a lower target of $80 if regional exports normalise, according to the bank. Brent currently trades near $98 a barrel. Diesel prices have more than doubled this year. The US reportedly struck three Iranian oil tankers and maintains a naval blockade of Iranian ports, while Tehran plans a new restricted maritime zone beyond the strait, stretching from the US blockade line into parts of the GCC.

Gulf operators are now pricing war risk into every supply chain and energy contract. The repeated targeting of Jazan signals that Saudi energy infrastructure is a priority target in the conflict, not a side effect. Any operator moving refined products or crude through the Gulf must treat the next 60 days as a period of elevated, unpredictable disruption. The safest assumption is that what happened to Jazan yesterday can happen again to any terminal on the Gulf coast. The war has stopped being a headline and become a cost center.

02PayTabs' Amazon deal
PayTabs Buys Amazon's MENA Payments
PayTabs Group agreed a $100 million-plus deal to buy Amazon Payment Services’ MENA operations. Amazon Payment Services, formerly PayFort, operates across nine MENA markets serving more than 3,500 businesses. The combined business is expected to process more than SAR 150 billion, around $40 billion, in transactions annually.

PayTabs was founded in 2014 by Abdulaziz Fahad Al-Jouf. The deal cements its position as the largest MENA payments infrastructure provider with end-to-end solutions. The acquisition turns a regional processor into the default rails for Amazon's former merchant base, a customer list no one else in the region could buy.

The deal creates a local payments champion at a time when regional digital commerce is growing despite the war. For Gulf merchants and fintechs, it means one fewer global heavyweight in the regional payments stack. The practical effect is that a Saudi-founded company now controls a piece of the payments pipeline that used to belong to a US giant. That is a shift in negotiating power, and it happened in one $100 million stroke. The region's payments pipes are now in regional hands.

03PIF's east coast bet
PIF Launches Al-Khafji Project
Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund launched the Gulf Coast Development Company to build a tourist and residential destination on the Al-Khafji coast in the Eastern Province. The project spans about 20 square kilometres with a 10-kilometre waterfront, eight residential neighbourhoods with 16,000 housing units, and 1,400 hotel keys. Development will happen in three phases, with the first three neighbourhoods due to finish by 2030.

This is the first project directly funded by PIF this year, and it falls under the urban development and liveability segment of the fund's 2026-30 strategy. That strategy makes no mention of The Line or Trojena, the two Neom developments that once dominated the fund's portfolio. Contractor awards in Saudi Arabia jumped 82% year-on-year in the first six months of 2026, according to Scavo, and Saad Alkroud was named head of local property investments at PIF.

The shift from megaprojects to smaller, phased, liveable developments is a signal to the construction market. PIF is still spending, but it is spending on projects that can open in years, not decades. For contractors and developers, the pipeline is moving from signature towers to functional neighbourhoods. The Al-Khafji project is the new shape of Saudi ambition: still big, but built to be finished. The era of endless giga-projects is quietly giving way to projects with a completion date.

Bitcoin holds near $80k
Bitcoin Braces for CPI
Bitcoin is holding near $80,000 despite renewed fears of a Federal Reserve rate hike. The odds of a September Fed rate increase returned to around 60% following Friday's jobs report. US inflation data, the CPI, is due later this week.

The crypto market is now trading in lockstep with macroeconomic data, not on its own narrative. The correlation between Bitcoin and the S&P 500 has tightened as institutional money dominates flows. For Gulf crypto investors, the Fed's path matters as much as any regional development.

The near-term test is whether Bitcoin can hold $80,000 into the CPI print. If inflation comes in hot, the rate hike odds climb and risk assets, including crypto, take a hit. For traders in the Gulf, the week ahead is a macro event, not a crypto event. The coin is just along for the ride. Bitcoin's next move will be decided in Washington, not in the market.

Insurance costs bite
UAE Event Insurance Soars
UAE event cancellation insurance costs have risen sharply due to regional conflict, with war cover for a $40 million event now costing about $5 million or more in premiums. Seven Insurance Brokers paid out "hundreds of millions" of dirhams in claims, including an AED 30 million payout this week. Boyd Edmondson of Seven Insurance Brokers says terrorism policies increasingly exclude regional militant groups since Israel's 2025 attack on Qatar, which he describes as a reported military action.

Dan Bolton of BE Creative says insurance costs increased 10-15% "across the board" from pre-conflict levels, according to him. Concerts including Irish band The Corrs, Christina Aguilera, and the Offlimits festival have been cancelled. Formula 1 events in Qatar and Abu Dhabi received the green light.

The squeeze is pushing organisers towards the UAE's own international community, as Katy Keenan of the British Chamber of Commerce Dubai notes. For event operators, the math is simple: the war has made the insurance premium the first line of every budget. The shows that go ahead are the ones with a local audience and no need for a war cover that costs more than the talent. The price of a live show in the Gulf now includes the cost of a war that no one can predict.

The AI Race
​
Nvidia Swallows Hugging Face
Nvidia agreed to acquire AI startup Hugging Face for $13 billion, according to a person familiar with the matter. The platform hosts 3 million models, 500,000 datasets, and 1 million applications. It is used by 18 million developers, researchers, and creators. About 200,000 companies use it to customise, develop, and deploy AI. The startup was valued at $4.5 billion in a funding round three years ago, so the price is a near tripling.

Jensen Huang committed to keeping Hugging Face open source and supporting open weight models and multi-cloud. Previous backers include Google, Amazon, Intel, and Salesforce. The deal consolidates the AI stack in a single chipmaker's hands. The neutral layer of open model hosting is no longer independent. Gulf AI builders should assume that every layer of the stack will eventually have a hardware parent. If you train or fine-tune models in the Gulf, your tooling now sits with the same company that sells the chips.

Dangote Targets $1.6bn IPO
Nigeria's Dangote Petroleum Refinery aims to raise about $1.63 billion by listing 4.1 billion ordinary shares at $0.40 each, Africa's largest-ever IPO. The refinery posted an after-tax profit of $1.82 billion in the first half of 2026, a sharp turnaround from a $476 million loss in 2025. The facility was built at a cost of about $20 billion and began operations in 2024. Adnoc is in talks to take a stake in Dangote's Nigerian operations.
UK-GCC Trade Deal Nears
The UK is ready to sign a trade agreement with the GCC, with the deal wrapped up in May after almost four years of negotiations. The UK estimates the agreement could lift trade with the bloc by 19.8% and eliminate $784 million in annual tariffs. UK-UAE trade reached £25 billion in 2025, almost half of the £53 billion traded between Britain and the GCC. The UAE-UK Sovereign Investment Partnership has delivered more than £30 billion of investment commitments into Britain since 2021.
Kuwait Ends Ban on Wealth Fund Borrowing
An Emiri decree now lets Kuwait's government tap its Future Generations Fund to support the treasury. Borrowing is subject to strict caps as fiscal pressures mount. This reverses a long-standing prohibition designed to protect the country's savings for future generations.
Six groups qualify for Qassim airport PPP
Saudi Arabia's Qassim airport project will be developed on a design, finance, construction, operations, maintenance and transfer basis. Six consortia have qualified for the 30-year PPP contract, with the airport located 25km from Buraidah. The expression of interest process launched in February.

Dubai's Holiday Homes Turn Long-Term
Short leisure bookings fell sharply after March, but monthly stays proved resilient. First Class' portfolio saw 87.1% occupancy, with an average stay of 21.3 nights against a market median of six. Dubai did not lose its guests; it got a different, longer-staying kind. The operator that survives is the one selling semi-permanent lodging to displaced families and remote workers, not a weekend to a tourist.
The Last Sip
​
Which Gulf city has more registered cats than cars?
The answer
Kuwait City has an enormous stray cat population, with estimates running to over a million. The city has tried feeding stations and neutering campaigns, and cats have become a fixture of parking lots and markets. They are so common that some neighbourhoods have more cats than parked cars. Nobody has formally counted either, but the cats are winning. The Gulf's urban planning challenge is now 60 percent whiskers.
Why is the Dangote refinery's IPO priced at $0.40 a share?
The answer
The refinery is listing 4.1 billion shares at $0.40 each to raise about $1.63 billion. That low per-share price keeps the share count enormous, which suits a retail investor base in Nigeria where affordability matters. It also makes the valuation near $49 billion look remarkably tidy. The low price is a feature, not a fire sale. At $0.40 a share, even the office coffee fund can be a refinery tycoon.
