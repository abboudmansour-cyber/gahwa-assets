The Lead · MACRO & GEO
A 56-tanker ghost fleet is moving Gulf oil right under Iran's nose.
​
The covert fleet was visually identified by tracking firm TankerTrackers.com. The 56 tankers are running without their digital location signals to move crude from Arab states through the Strait of Hormuz, according to the firm's report, performing ship-to-ship transfers once they reach the relative safety of the Gulf of Oman.

The dark fleet is the operational answer to Iran's security chief, who said Saturday the strait would stay closed until Washington ended hostile policies, withdrew its naval forces, and lifted sanctions. Hours later, the UAE reported that, according to its account, an Iranian missile hit a vessel linked to its state oil company ADNOC. The foreign ministry labeled it an act of piracy.

Iraq is making its own demand-driven argument. Baghdad agreed with a Chevron-led consortium to build a $15 billion pipeline to Turkey with a daily capacity of 2 million barrels, its own pre-war exports having cratered from 3.4 million bpd through the strait to less than half that today. The country says it has lost more than $40 billion in revenue since the conflict began.
What to do with it
The fleet's emergence is the clearest sign yet of how a protracted closure is reshaping the physical route of global oil trade.

05GCC & SAUDI
Moove Hits $2.1B Value on Waymo Fleet Bet
Moove raised a $250 million Series C led by Mubadala at a $2.1 billion valuation. The mobility fintech generates $420 million in annual recurring revenue across 42,000 vehicles in 13 countries and now wants its autonomous workforce to swell by 220%, from 150 to 500 employees by year end.

Its partnership with Waymo is already live in Phoenix and Miami, with London next on the list. Toyota's growth fund co-led the round. The company's bet is that financing a vehicle's brain is eventually a much larger business than financing its engine, and it is staffing up to plug an AV labor shortage before anyone calls it a labor market. Mubadala's lead bet on a mobility fintech suggests the smart Gulf money is moving from backing apps to owning the fleet layer.

FINTECH & CRYPTO
Bitcoin Splinters Over BIP-110 Chain Fork
Bitcoin's BIP-110 soft fork triggered a minority chain split as the main network pulled ahead with hardly any miner backing. The proposal, which would limit non-financial data on the blockchain for one year, drew less than 3% support from miners.

The debate is not about a code bug but about what the chain is for: a permissionless database or a pure payments rail. BIP-110 supporters effectively forked themselves into a small, ideologically clean corner of the network while the rest of Bitcoin kept humming past block 961,632. The fork is a live case study in how a deeply divided validator set resolves a clash over chain purpose, with no single actor able to force consensus.

AI & TECH
Amazon's Texas Data Hub May Pollute Most
Amazon is building an on-site power plant for a planned Texas data center that could become the largest single source of climate pollution in the United States. The cloud giant is not merely plugging into the local grid but generating its own, at a scale that sets a new national record for a private facility.

The project is a live answer to what happens when AI's electricity appetite hits the scale where green grids can't keep up. Rather than wait for infrastructure that doesn't exist, Amazon chose to produce its own power and its own emissions, presenting the AI buildout with an environmental price tag its balance sheet is not the one holding. A Gulf operator with its own gigawatt-scale AI data center plans should read this as an early look at the permitting and PR war ahead.

GCC & SAUDI
PDO Tenders Flare Gas Monetisation Scheme
Oman's state oil firm PDO issued a tender for a major scheme to turn flare and associated gas from its Qarn Alam cluster and Fahud field into a revenue stream instead of a carbon liability. The project sits within the company's northern Block 6 concession and is one part of a wider push to stop burning gas at the wellhead.

Flare gas has moved from an embarrassment to a line item in the Gulf's energy transition budget. Oman is openly pricing the cost of lighting the sky on fire every night and betting that the gas in those flares is worth more in a pipeline than on a satellite photo. The tender signals a broader Gulf operational pivot: flare gas is being treated as a stranded asset no one can afford to leave stranded any longer.

PRODUCT
Syria's First Tourism Super App Lands
Four UAE-based Syrian founders launched My Syria, the country's first tourism super app that bundles hotels, restaurants, transport, and digital payments into one platform. It is also Syria's first platform to support cross-border payments via Apple Pay, Google Pay, Visa, and Mastercard, a banking workaround for a country still mostly cut off from global financial rails.

The launch arrived as Syria's visitor numbers jumped 111% in the first half of 2026 to 3.52 million. The Ministry of Tourism put its patronage behind the app. The founders seem to have noticed that millions of arrivals with dollars and no way to tap their phone is not a tourism market but a very large cash pile waiting for its first payment terminal. A Gulf fintech team just put the first POS in a market that doubled its tourist count in a year, which is a land grab disguised as an app launch.

Shaffra Lands in Baku, Skirts Silicon Valley
UAE-founded enterprise AI startup Shaffra opened its first headquarters outside the GCC in Azerbaijan, choosing a market with roughly 200 startups and a government that wants energy-efficient AI. The firm says it saved enterprises 2 million work hours a month in 2025 with a deeply unglamorous product: an AI call center agent that picks up the phone. It has raised more than $10 million from stc, Omantel and global tech investors.
What it means here
Shaffra's move signals that Gulf AI startups are finding their first scaling path in underserved non-Western markets rather than fighting for a slot in Sand Hill Road.

A Housemaid, a VIP Ticket, and a Sold-Out Arena
Lilibeth Acista Barnachea, a 48-year-old housemaid in Abu Dhabi, asked her employer for a day off to see the Filipino group SB Girls and was given a VIP ticket to the Etihad Arena. The 19-member group became the first act from the Philippines to play the venue, and for one night, a domestic worker's name sat inside a VIP section because an employer decided loyalty was worth a literal front-row seat.
The arena sellout shows the Gulf's Filipino workforce is now a cultural market big enough to anchor a major tour, and one employer turned a concert ticket into the best retention tool a salary can't buy.
