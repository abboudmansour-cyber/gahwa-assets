Dubai landlords are learning the hardest lesson in real estate: the power was never in the marble, it was in the scarcity. Betterhomes reports monthly rent payments doubled this year, from 2% of tenancies to 5%. The city delivered a record 27,300 homes in one quarter. So the landlord who once demanded a year upfront now accepts a monthly cheque, and the one-cheque guarantee is becoming a niche product. Call it the end of Dubai's longest-running game of chicken.

The Lead
​
Dubai's airport lost a third of its passengers to a war it never joined, and rents are falling so fast the city is teaching landlords to accept monthly cheques.
Dubai International Airport handled 31.5 million passengers in the first half of 2026, a 31.3% drop from the same period last year. The US-Iran war kept travellers away from the region's main transit hub. Aircraft movements fell 32.1% to 150,600, and air freight volumes dropped 28.7% to around 751,000 tonnes.

The recovery is already visible in the monthly data. Passenger numbers climbed from 3.5 million in April to 5 million in June. Emirates flew at 90% capacity in July with premium cabins full, and ranked first globally for international capacity in August despite a 5.4% year-on-year capacity drop.

UAE entry permits fell from 1.94 million in February to 647,944 in April, a drop of two thirds, before climbing back to 1 million by July. Marriott, Hilton and Hyatt all cited the war for double-digit declines in revenue per available room in the Middle East during Q2. Accor's CFO said UAE activity was down about 80% year on year in April, recovering to a 40-45% decline by June.

The World Travel and Tourism Council forecasts the UAE will lose 46,000 tourism roles this year, part of a wider 137,000 across the Gulf. Middle East travel and tourism activity is the only region expected to shrink in 2026, contracting 14.5%. Dubai Airports chief executive Paul Griffiths called the first half a test of "every part of the aviation system." The monthly improvement suggests the worst is over, but the tourism job losses will echo through the Gulf economy for the rest of the year.

02Hamas disarmament
Hamas agrees to surrender arms
According to officials, Hamas and other armed factions in Gaza agreed for the first time to surrender their weapons and transfer full civilian and security authority to a UN-recognized transitional administration. The announcement came from Nikolay Mladenov, the Gaza Peace Board's high representative, at a UN Security Council briefing. The agreement is part of the implementation of the US-backed Gaza peace plan.

The deal would mark the first time Hamas has formally agreed to disarm, a condition Israel and the US have demanded for any lasting ceasefire. Gulf states that have pledged reconstruction funds will watch closely to see whether the transition holds. Saudi Arabia has made clear that Gaza's reconstruction and a Palestinian state are central to its regional strategy. If the disarmament holds, reconstruction financing from Gulf states could begin to flow, but the pledge still needs to survive contact with reality.

03Tim Curry dies
Tim Curry dies at 80
Tim Curry, the English actor who defined a generation's villain in "The Rocky Horror Picture Show" and terrified children as Pennywise in "It," reportedly died Tuesday at his Los Angeles home at age 80. His manager confirmed he passed away peacefully at his Toluca Lake residence. Curry had used a wheelchair since a severe stroke in 2012.

Susan Sarandon, Nell Campbell, Luke Evans, Richard O'Brien and Carol Burnett were among those who paid tribute. O'Brien, who wrote "The Rocky Horror Show," said "the head of our family has just died," according to the family's announcement. For Gulf audiences who grew up on VHS copies of "Rocky Horror" and late-night re-runs, Curry's death closes a distinctive chapter of 1980s pop culture nostalgia. He leaves behind a body of work that still populates Halloween parties and midnight screenings in Dubai and beyond.

04Swvl's $13M PIPE
Swvl raises $13 million
Swvl secured a $13 million private investment in public equity led by Coefficient LP, backed by Egypt's Sawiris family, which will invest $10 million and become Swvl's largest institutional shareholder. Swvl's Q1 2026 revenue grew 68% year-over-year to $8.2 million, with GCC revenue up 111%. Recurring revenue stands at 88% of total, and net dollar retention is 114%. Operating expenses fell to 23% of revenue, putting the company near operating breakeven.

The transport platform, founded by Mostafa Kandil, operates in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, the UK and the US. The new capital will accelerate US expansion and launch a lending offering for transport operators. Onsi Sawiris said the investment reflects confidence in Kandil's leadership. The deal shows Egyptian tech companies can still draw Gulf and global capital despite a tough funding environment. For regional transport startups, Swvl's comeback is a template for surviving a public listing gone wrong and rebuilding around recurring revenue.

Bitcoin hits $80k
Bitcoin's August surge to $80k confirmed
Bitcoin surged to $80,000 in August 2026, and a lesser-tracked indicator is now flashing green, confirming that capital moved consistently in size to push prices higher. The indicator tracks whether large holders are accumulating rather than distributing.

The move to $80,000 puts Bitcoin within striking distance of its high, but the rally is broader than a single asset. Fasset, an Islamic crypto neobank that recently raised at a $1 billion valuation, partnered with Tether on a gold-backed card paying up to 6% cashback in XAUt. Gulf crypto adoption continues to grow, with local exchanges and fintechs offering sharia-compliant products to a market that was once hesitant. For Gulf investors, Bitcoin at $80,000 is less interesting than the institutional products now coming out of regional exchanges and fintechs.

The AI Race
​
OpenAI's own chip beats Nvidia
OpenAI's first chip, code-named Jalapeño, beat Nvidia's flagship systems on speed and power efficiency in benchmarks released today. The 700-watt chip topped Nvidia's 1,200-watt options, answering up to 3.6 times faster with up to 1.9 times more work per watt. OpenAI's hardware VP said the company will not sell the chip because of enormous internal demand, and it will still lean on Nvidia to train new models. The chip took just nine months from first design to manufacturing-ready, with two more generations already in development.

This is a direct challenge to Nvidia's dominance, and it comes from the company that is also Nvidia's biggest customer. Anthropic, Amazon, Microsoft and Google are all designing their own silicon to cut the cost of AI inference, which OpenAI says could be roughly 50% lower than Nvidia's current best chips. Nvidia's response is to double down on financing platforms and residual value guarantees, like the $105 billion one for OpenAI's Ohio data center, which now looks like an insurance policy on a customer that is quietly becoming a competitor. Gulf data centres planning to host AI workloads will soon choose between Nvidia and a growing list of rivals, a price war that could make AI infrastructure much cheaper to build.

Off Paper
​
Saudi adults hit 2030 fitness target early
Saudi Arabia reported that 59.1% of adults met the recommended 150 minutes of weekly physical activity in 2025, beating the 2025 target of 55%. Physical activity among children aged 5-17 reached 19%, a target originally set for 2030. This is a concrete win for Vision 2030's sports investment. It shows the Kingdom's fitness push is working, and it is why boutique brands like the jiu-jitsu network GMNSM are betting on Riyadh.
A jiu-jitsu academy plans Riyadh expansion
GMNSM, a jiu-jitsu academy network with 11 academies across the UAE, Qatar and Cyprus, raised $2 million from VEYRA Capital. The company serves more than 700 children and adults and plans to open its first academy in Riyadh and its first in Monaco. It also built its own lesson audit platform called Allim AI. It is a small but telling sign that niche fitness is now a venture-backed business in the Gulf, not just a hobby.
