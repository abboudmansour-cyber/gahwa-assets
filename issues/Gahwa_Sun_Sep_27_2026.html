The Lead

War chills Saudi expat jobs
Saudi Arabia's expatriate jobs boom is fading as the Iran war deepens a hiring slowdown. Recruiters report fewer openings, tighter salary offers and an influx of laid-off candidates entering the market. The slowdown comes after a long stretch in which the Kingdom's giga-projects and economic transformation pulled in international talent at a fast clip.

The war's effect on the jobs market is a second-order cost that rarely makes the headline count. As projects deferred or restructured timelines and the oil supply shock tightened budgets, employers pulled back on new requisitions and started renegotiating packages. Chinese institutional investors are also moving into high-grade Gulf debt, drawn by higher yields, while Chinese banks take a larger role in primary bond issuance.

For operators, the signal is the GCC's labour story is no longer an automatic growth curve. The candidates are still there, but the roles are fewer and the leverage has shifted to the employer. That shift shows up fastest in offers for roles that had been bid up fiercely during the post-2022 transformation wave. A market that spent years fighting over talent is now watching the same talent queue up for fewer cards on the table.

02Coal, toys and car seats
US-China tariff truce extended
The US and China traded tariff cuts on $30 billion of goods, covering $30 billion of non-sensitive goods from each side, and extended their trade truce for two months, pushing the deadline from November 10 into January 2027. US products covered include agricultural goods, fish and seafood, logs and wood products, cosmetics and medical devices. Chinese goods include small appliances, toys, holiday decorations and children's car seats.

The deal also created a working group on agricultural market-access barriers and a new US-China Super Intelligence Dialogue, with the next exchange expected by November 2026. China committed to import at least 10 million metric tons of US coal in both 2027 and 2028. The two leaders formally agreed that Iran can never have a nuclear weapon and that no country should impose tolls on international waterways.

For Gulf traders, the tariff thaw takes some pressure off the global demand picture at a moment when Brent is near $97 and shipping costs are already elevated. A calmer US-China trade relationship means steadier Asian demand for Gulf exports, and less volatility in the currency and commodity channels that Gulf economies depend on. When the world's two largest importers start cutting tariffs, the Gulf's biggest customer relationships get a little less expensive to defend.

03The bill arrives first
AI pushed hospital costs up
A rare $942 million AI cost figure from Blue Cross Blue Shield: hospital use of AI tools led to an additional $942 million in healthcare spending over a two-year period. The insurer's claim is a rare concrete number in a global AI debate dominated by promises of efficiency. The cost increase comes as health systems add AI for diagnostics, documentation and patient monitoring.

The finding matters well beyond American insurers. Gulf governments are pouring money into AI-driven healthcare under national health transformation programmes, and hospital operators in the region are being pitched the same tools. The BCBS number suggests that early adoption can raise costs before it lowers them, a pattern that will sound familiar to any operator who watched digital health hype meet clinical reality.

The deeper lesson is that AI in a regulated, high-stakes sector does not follow the software playbook. It adds workflows, oversight and infrastructure before it removes anything. A Gulf hospital buying AI tools next quarter will pay the same entry costs, and the question is whether anyone has budgeted for the bill after the pilot ends. A health system that assumes AI saves money from day one is about to learn the difference between a pilot and a line item.

PIF weighs first Pimco mandate for Gulf debt
The sovereign fund is considering an initial $500 million mandate for Pimco to buy mainly Gulf government bonds. It would be PIF's first mandate to the bond giant. Gulf sovereign debt has sold off since the war began. Investors are now demanding 121 basis points more in yield across the region.
Aramco targets 4 million barrels daily from repaired pipeline
The East-West Pipeline restarted Tuesday at a reduced rate after a drone strike reportedly hit three pumping stations. Aramco wants to hit nearly 4 million barrels per day and 40 percent of capacity within days. Full restart is expected in six to eight weeks.
ADIA buys into KAUST $1 billion secondaries sale
The Abu Dhabi Investment Authority has bought into the university's private markets portfolio sale worth over $1 billion. The assets carry Asia-Pacific and China exposure. Jefferies is advising on the transaction.
US pitches $10 billion Gulf energy rebuild fund
Washington is seeking matching contributions from eight regional states to create a $10 billion fund for energy infrastructure damaged in the Iran war. The proposal would pool Gulf and American money for reconstruction. It remains a pitch, not a commitment.
Saudi Arabia Railways signs phosphate rail deals
The rail operator signed agreements with Alstom, Progress Rail and Greenbrier for phosphate haulage lines. The deals cover rolling stock and related rail equipment. They link mining output to export ports, moving freight off trucks and onto rail.

Off Paper

Preserving a football legend's memories
A Dubai dementia support group called 4get-me-not is helping a UAE football legend and others living with cognitive decline to preserve their memories. The group works with patients to document their stories, giving families a record that outlasts the disease's progress. Dementia remains a quiet crisis in the Gulf, where families often carry the care burden privately. The things a football legend forgets are not his own losses; they are the stories a family will never get back.
A five-hundred-year feud on screen
Gulf News is revisiting Sanjay Leela Bhansali's 2013 film Goliyon Ki Rasleela Ram-Leela, a Bollywood retelling of Romeo and Juliet set in a fictional Gujarati town where two clans have traded bullets for 500 years. The rewind is timed ahead of Bhansali's next film Love & War. For Gulf audiences, Bollywood remains cultural bedrock. Bollywood's appeal in the Gulf was never just about songs and stars; it was about the scale of the spectacle.
A football club builds a neighbourhood
Ohana, a gated community that will be Manchester City's first branded residential project worldwide, has started construction in Abu Dhabi. The development will carry the club's brand across its homes and amenities. The construction start moves a paper announcement into the ground. When a football club's brand moves from shirts to street addresses, the pitch has become a property play.

The Laughter Factory turns 30
Dubai's Laughter Factory, the stand-up club that gave Jack Whitehall and Kevin Bridges their first overseas gigs, turns 30 this year. Co-founded in 1996, it also hosted Joe Lycett and Jason Manford before they became arena acts. Three decades ago a comedy night in Dubai was a strange bet; now the city is a stop on every major tour. The harder trick was doing it in 1996, before the audiences, the venues and the talent agencies existed.
