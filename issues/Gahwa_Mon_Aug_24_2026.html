The Lead
US promises an 'economic D-Day' on Iran while Iran threatens to choke off every barrel leaving the Gulf.
​
Iran's answer is to threaten what it has always held in reserve, reportedly shutting down all oil exports from the Gulf. That threat now hangs over roughly a fifth of the world's crude flows, most of it moving through the Strait of Hormuz. The US says the sanctions are aimed at forcing a change in Iranian behaviour. Iran says the economic war leaves it no choice but to respond in kind.

For Gulf states, the threat is economic infrastructure, not just geopolitics. Shipping insurers, port operators, and every exporter that moves goods through Hormuz now has to price in a scenario they spent the past decade hoping was off the table. That repricing is already showing up in freight and insurance quotes. The region's ability to keep goods moving is now a card in someone else's negotiation. The Gulf's worst-case scenario just became an explicit negotiating position in a sanctions standoff.

LEAP 2026 Is Riyadh's AI Reckoning
LEAP 2026's fifth edition opens in Riyadh at the end of the month, and the numbers already carry the ambition. Since the first edition in 2022, Saudi Arabia has drawn over $42.4 billion in tech infrastructure investment. Last year opened with $14.9 billion in AI announcements on day one.

The policy machinery behind it has thickened. Saudi Arabia designated this year its Year of Artificial Intelligence. HUMAIN launched in 2025 to build across the AI stack from data centres to applications. SDAIA has trained 14,495 data and AI specialists against a 20,000 target for 2030. The SAMAI initiative has passed one million citizens trained in AI.

The UAE is running a parallel track. Its National Artificial Intelligence System advises the Cabinet. The new Artificial Intelligence and Data Authority wants a unified digital government using agentic AI. Both Gulf states are pouring money into the same bet. As IDC warns, more than 90% of global organisations will feel an IT skills crisis by 2026, with $5.5 trillion in losses attached. The country that trains the most people fastest wins the next decade. The next Gulf race is not for capital, but for the coders and AI specialists who make the capital productive.

Middle East Tourism Lost A Year
Regional inbound arrivals are expected to fall 32% this year even if the ceasefire holds, against an earlier forecast of 14% growth. That is a swing of nearly 50 percentage points in the wrong direction.

Gulf hubs move about 14% of global transit traffic and roughly a fifth of Europe-Asia travel. Bookings through major Gulf hubs for the second and third quarters fell by more than 40%. Middle Eastern carriers cancelled 46,000 flights between late February and 11 March alone. March saw carriers flying about half as many flights as the year before. April capacity was cut by more than a third.

The recovery is not expected to be quick. If the ceasefire holds, arrivals should bounce 51% in 2027. If hostilities resume first, the rebond is 81%. Saudi Arabia is already tightening one part of the cross-border flow: GCC-registered private vehicles will be limited to 90-day stays starting Wednesday. Overstaying will cost up to SAR 2,000 plus impoundment. The tourism machine is not broken, but it is no longer the one the region planned for. The region's tourism recovery math now depends on a single variable: whether the guns stay silent long enough for the rebound to start.

Saudi Construction Cools To Four-Month Low
Saudi Arabia's construction cost index rose 2.3% in July, its slowest pace in four months, down from 2.7% in June. Residential costs rose 2.2%, non-residential 2.6%.

The slowdown masks a split. Energy prices rose 3% in July. Equipment rental costs jumped 5.8% for non-residential projects, while rentals with operators surged 7.3%. Timber and joinery rose 3.9% on the residential side. The lines tied to conflict still run hot. Seven in ten contractors told Meed their input prices have risen up to 25% since the war began.

The cooler headline number reflects Saudi Arabia's shifting project mix, with spending now tilted toward Expo 2030, the 2034 World Cup, and rail. Those are multi-year builds with different cost profiles than the gigaproject boom. For anyone budgeting a project in the Kingdom, the message is that the biggest cost risks are now concentrated in a few war-sensitive categories, not spread across the board. The cost risk is no longer in the headline index; it is hiding inside equipment rental and energy lines.

UAE Probes Binance Fund Flows
UAE police detained two Binance employees at airports in recent weeks and questioned a third, including one held overnight in Sharjah. Reuters traced at least $4 billion moved since May 2024 through the unlicensed Dubai exchange Shelbit, with roughly $676 million flowing from Shelbit-linked addresses into Binance.

Dubai's Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority has already ordered Shelbit to shut down. Binance says the detentions were routine inquiries into third-party fund flows through a client money account. The exchange holds three licenses from Abu Dhabi Global Market and took a $2 billion investment from state-backed MGX in March 2025.

The probe is a reminder that even licensed exchanges face hard questions about what moves through their pipes. Binance's founder Changpeng Zhao calls Dubai home, and the emirate has spent years building a reputation as a serious crypto regulator. That reputation now depends on how this investigation concludes. Dubai's crypto rulebook is only as credible as the enforcement that follows it.

Dubai Schools Steady to the Bell
Gems Education held retention at around 85% while Nord Anglia saw over 90% of students return. Taaleem's premium schools added about 4,000 students, enough to absorb 10% annual churn. Teacher turnover at Gems fell to 16-17%, the lowest in a decade, against the 25% international norm, and Taaleem lost only 9% of staff. The price tag was real: Taaleem's nurseries lost AED 30 million in net income and Dubai froze private school fees for the academic year. For Gulf families, the schools held, but the stability was paid for somewhere.

The AI Race
​
The global race, landed on the Gulf.
​
Altman Says AI Field Blew the Narrative
Sam Altman admitted on a podcast that AI builders have done a poor job explaining the technology's benefits and mitigations. After years of extinction-risk talk and job-loss warnings, he said the field never learned to sell hope. His new pitch: AI gives people more power and will spark the greatest boom in small business creation ever seen. Saudi Arabia and the UAE are spending billions on national AI strategies that depend on public trust, so repairing that narrative is now part of the Gulf's AI bet.
