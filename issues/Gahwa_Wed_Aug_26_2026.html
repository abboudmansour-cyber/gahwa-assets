The Lead
​
Saudi Arabia's trade surplus reportedly fell for the first time since February, and the culprit is hiding in plain sight.
Japan remained the top export destination at 13.2%, followed by South Korea at 11.5% and China at 9.4%. China led imports at 22% ahead of Switzerland and the United States. The gap between what the Kingdom sells and buys is still wide, but it is narrowing faster than many expected.

Plastics and rubber were the largest non-oil export category at 20.7% of the total, falling 12.8% year-on-year. Chemical products declined 30.4%. The ratio of non-oil exports to imports slipped to 34.9% from 37.5% a year earlier, which means local producers are covering a smaller share of what the country buys from abroad.

The broader context is the Hormuz war. The IMF says five of eight Gulf oil exporters directly affected are facing outright economic contractions this year. Oil prices are running 30% above pre-war levels, but that has not been enough to offset export volume risk. Saudi Arabia's oil share of total exports actually rose to 72% from 70.4% because non-oil exports fell faster than oil did. That is the opposite of what Vision 2030 needs.

Saudi policymakers want 70% homeownership by the end of the decade and a non-oil economy that can stand on its own. The June trade data shows the distance between that ambition and the current footing. Operators in logistics, manufacturing, and services should read this as a signal that government-backed spending will stay defensive until the trade account stabilizes. The trade account is not yet a reason to panic, but it is a clear sign that diversification targets are still hostage to the oil price cycle.

Aramco Signs $3.7B French Deals
Aramco signed $3.7 billion worth of agreements with French companies during a French-Saudi investment roundtable in Paris. The deals were signed by Aramco and its digital arm, Aramco Digital. Amin Nasser, the company's president and CEO, attended the meeting. The agreements cover areas that align with the Kingdom's push into technology, services, and industrial capability.

The roundtable was part of a broader deepening of Saudi-French investment ties. Qiddiya, the Saudi entertainment developer, also signed a memorandum to build up to three theme parks near Paris at an estimated cost of $7 billion. These moves are not random. Riyadh is trying to anchor its giga-projects and industrial strategy to established European partners.

For Gulf operators, the Aramco deals signal where the oil giant is placing its next bets. The company is moving beyond upstream oil and into digital infrastructure, AI, and downstream services. The French agreements are likely to generate subcontracting opportunities for local firms over the next two years. Every time Aramco signs a framework deal, a supply chain follows. French industrial partners are now bound to Aramco's spending cycle, and that creates a queue of work for Gulf contractors.

Stellaria Raises $6.8M Seed
UAE-based Stellaria, formerly Farmin, raised $6.8 million in a seed round from angel investors at a valuation of $114.4 million. The Abu Dhabi company builds an AI-powered operating system for geospatial intelligence. Its platform covers satellite imagery analytics, automated target recognition, maritime and port intelligence, and environmental monitoring.

The founding team is led by Ali AlHammadi and the company is an alumnus of the Mohammed Bin Rashid Innovation Fund's Innovation Accelerator Programme. The funding will go toward developing AI and geospatial technologies, expanding the technical team, and scaling deployments across the UAE and international markets. The applications span government, defence, maritime, ports, infrastructure, and environmental monitoring.

For Gulf operators, this is a signal that the UAE's defence and logistics sectors are now buying AI-native tools from local startups rather than importing everything from Western primes. Stellaria's seed round is small, but its valuation of over $100 million means investors are pricing in a regional intelligence platform with government customers already in the pipeline. A geospatial AI company at a $114 million seed valuation is less a startup and more a bet that the Gulf wants its own eyes in the sky.

SpaceX Builds $100B Louisiana Spaceport
SpaceX will invest $100 billion to build a spaceport in Louisiana on about 125,000 acres in Vermilion Parish. The project, called Starbase Louisiana, will be the company's fourth and largest launch site. Construction is expected to begin in 2027. First launches are targeted as early as 2029. The facility will feature five launch complexes, each with two launch pads.

The project is expected to create 3,000 direct jobs over the next decade with average annual salaries of about $92,600. Louisiana will receive $25 million annually for 25 years under a payment-in-lieu-of-taxes agreement, plus a $20 million upfront payment. SpaceX reportedly completed the largest IPO in history earlier this year, raising $85.7 billion at a valuation above $1.75 trillion. A private company building at this scale resets what public space agencies and state-backed hubs must match. Saudi Arabia's space economy is projected to nearly quadruple to $31.6 billion by 2035, but SpaceX is now the private yardstick Gulf spaceports will be measured against.

The Quiet Landlord Behind Saudi Shelves
Raff, a Saudi retailtech startup founded in 2024, has helped more than 900 brands manage their physical shelf space across nine countries. It handles distribution, inventory, orders, and payments so brands can sell in stores without hiring a full distribution team. Its $1.7 million pre-seed round drew Vision Ventures, 500 Global, Palm VC, Oqal Group, and Salla co-founder Salman Butt. The GCC retail market is worth more than $300 billion a year, and now local software is quietly running its back end.
