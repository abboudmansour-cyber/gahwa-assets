The Lead

PIF May Exit LIV Golf Bankruptcy
Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund is preparing to walk away from LIV Golf, the most expensive sports experiment the Gulf has ever funded. BC Partners is preparing a preliminary bid for the $50 million debtor-in-possession loan the Public Investment Fund holds. That loan carries control of LIV's Chapter 11 case. PIF owned 98% of LIV and stopped funding it in September.

Why it is ending now LIV Golf is set to wind down, with liquidation paperwork due Monday. PIF would put in more money to settle trade vendor claims that could run past $100 million. Just $2 million is set aside for unsecured creditors. BC Partners' bid would preserve $5 billion in net operating losses LIV has racked up in the US and UK since 2021. The firm is pursuing a 2027 relaunch, thinner than the 17-tournament, $300 million version once proposed.

The kingdom's other numbers are tightening at the same moment. Saudi Arabia posted a SAR 244.3 billion deficit in the first nine months of the year, against an original full-year forecast of SAR 165.4 billion. Revenues rose 3% to SAR 858.9 billion, while grants jumped 197% and subsidies rose 49%.

A quieter exit This is not how the PIF imagined closing the book on golf. The fund backed a breakaway league, forced a merger, and now lets the bankruptcy process decide the value of the asset. The kingdom got the disruption it paid for; the question is what that disruption is worth without the patron. The kingdom wrote the checks for nearly a decade; now it is learning what the asset is worth without its patron.

02Checking out of demand deposits
Saudi Banks Face Years of Pricier Funding
Saudi banks face years of pricier funding as lending outpaces deposits, and Fitch has put the number on the squeeze. Bank credit reached SAR 3.5 trillion in September, up 7% year on year, while deposits and broad money each grew about 5%. The non-regulatory loan-to-deposit ratio rose to around 114% from 111%.

The cheap deposits are leaving. Time and savings deposits jumped 23% year on year, while cheaper demand deposits fell 5% and dropped to 46% of total deposits from 50% a year earlier. Public-sector enterprise lending rose 21.5% year on year to SAR 285.1 billion in September, roughly three times total credit growth.

The pressure is not ending Fitch senior director Anton Lopatin says deposit competition is likely to remain the most immediate pressure. Funding costs are set to stay elevated even if policy rates fall. Margins are compressing most at banks with price-sensitive deposits, weak CASA franchises, rapid loan growth, heavy wholesale reliance, or large fixed-rate books. Reliance on wholesale funding is likely to grow via international bonds, sukuk, and syndicated loans. Fitch's ratio has moved from 111% to about 114%, which is not a crisis but is not a rounding error either.

03Rail Deal Signed
GCC Inks Regional Railway Agreement
Gulf ministers signed the General Agreement for the GCC Railway Project in Bahrain on Sunday. It is the first formal framework for a line first proposed decades ago. The 2,117-kilometre line is 55.9% complete and targets full operations by December 2030. It is projected to carry more than 95 million tons of goods a year by 2045. Each country finances and tenders its own section.

The money is already moving Hafeet Rail, the $2.5 billion, 238-kilometre line linking Oman's Sohar port to the UAE network, has secured $1.5 billion in debt financing through a joint venture of Etihad Rail, Oman's Asyad Group, and Mubadala. Reportedly, the route matters more than the ribbon: A rail network that links Gulf ports without touching the strait Iran keeps threatening to shut has strategic value beyond cargo volumes. The project gives each Gulf state a stake in a corridor that bypasses the one maritime chokepoint that has made every shipping planner nervous since February. The railway is not only about trade; it is about what happens to trade when the water route closes. Reportedly, a rail network that links Gulf ports without touching the strait Iran keeps threatening to shut changes the calculus for every logistics operator in the region.

04Trade Eases
UAE Trade Hits Record Near AED 3 Trillion
UAE non-oil foreign trade grew 10% year on year to almost AED 3 trillion in the first nine months of 2026. That is a record for the period. The first half grew 13.1%, implying a third-quarter expansion of about 5%, the slowest pace this year. Non-oil exports to the UAE's ten largest trading partners rose 31%.

The war is leaving a mark. The UAE non-oil economy shrank 1.1% in the second quarter as war disrupted trade, and the third-quarter slowdown shows the drag continued. Trade remained the largest contributor to non-oil GDP in the first half at 16.2%. The country has concluded CEPAs with 40 countries, 20 of them in force, and is targeting AED 4 trillion in non-oil foreign trade by 2031. The record looks impressive until you see the quarterly trend, which is pointing to the one thing the UAE cannot sign an agreement with.

05Strait arithmetic
Physical Gulf Crude Climbs Above Futures
Brent futures traded at $97.49 a barrel, down 83 cents, while Murban physical crude rose $3.28 to $99.17, about $1.70 above Brent futures. The gap shows how the Iran war is pricing risk into actual barrels delivered from the Gulf.

The Strait factor IMF PortWatch recorded one vessel transit on November 1, against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 per day. A tanker was reported burning after a strike in the Strait of Hormuz on November 8. The paper market is calm and the physical market is not, and the gap between them is the price of the war.
