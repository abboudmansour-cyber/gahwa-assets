The Lead

Saudi listings stall as money pivots to debt
The Saudi Capital Market Authority approved no main-market IPOs in 2Q this year, after clearing three in 1Q, while the Nomu parallel market saw only three listings. There are 31 requests still under review, down from 51 a year earlier, and the CMA is now drafting rules that would require bookbuilding orders to be backed by existing liquidity.

Fund flows are following the same cautious line. Net subscriptions to Saudi public funds fell 60% year on year to SAR 13 billion in 1H 2026, down from SAR 32.7 billion a year earlier. Debt funds led inflows at SAR 5.2 billion and money market funds became the largest category at SAR 87.4 billion, overtaking equity funds at SAR 43.7 billion. Equity funds saw SAR 0.8 billion in net outflows.

Why the queue for capital is already priced in Total public fund assets still rose 19% to SAR 245.5 billion across 371 instruments, so the money did not leave the system. It just moved into shorter, safer paper while conflict reshapes the region. The same dynamic is visible in debt funds, which grew 58.6% year on year. For firms waiting on a listing or raising on the main market, the window is not closed, but the buyers have shifted toward yield and parked cash, and a listing that cannot show immediate liquidity is now a harder sell. Equity funds lost SAR 0.8 billion while money market funds reached SAR 87.4 billion.

02Shipping shock
Supertanker costs hit record as war reroutes oil
The cost to charter a supertanker from the US to Asia reached a record $77 million, up more than 730% from the $9.2 million average in 2025, per industry data. For a typical 2 million barrel cargo, that freight charge alone adds about $38.50 per barrel to delivery costs, and West African sellers are already discounting crude at the point of export to compensate.

War-risk insurance is compounding the bill. Premiums for Hormuz transits have climbed from 0.2% to 1% of hull value, pushing the insurance cost for a $150 million VLCC from $300,000 to $1.5 million per voyage. The Strait of Hormuz still handles about 20% of global oil flows, and Gulf volumes excluding Iran have recovered to only 81% of pre-war levels.

The EIA raised its 2026 Brent forecast to $98 per barrel on average, and now sees Q4 at $105 per barrel, which is $14 higher than its prior estimate. Middle East oil flows are expected to stay constrained through the fourth quarter. For Gulf producers and buyers, the spread between regional crude and delivered barrels has become the single most important pricing input, and every day the corridor stays risky widens it. What used to be a nuisance cost of cargo is now a structural surcharge on the entire region's energy trade. A $38.50 per barrel freight charge makes West African crude cheaper at the wellhead and more expensive in Asia.

03War rebuild
UAE economy shrinks while non-oil defies war
The UAE economy contracted 2.1% year on year in 2Q 2026 to AED 476.9 billion, with oil GDP down around 5 to 6% and even non-oil activity down 1.1% as the war hit tourism. That follows a 1Q in which GDP was up 3% and non-oil expanded 4.8%, before the conflict began in late February.

Across the first half, non-oil GDP still grew 1.8% and lifted its share of the economy to 79.2% from 78.1% a year earlier. Financial and insurance activities led growth at 14.8%, followed by information and communications at 7.3% and construction at 5.1%. The UAE non-oil private-sector PMI slipped to 50.8 in June, the weakest June reading in over five years.

The World Bank expects the whole GCC to contract 4.3% in 2026, and sees the UAE down 1.6% for the full year. Dubai is already 90% cashless and government cashless collections hit 99.7% in 1H 2026, which speaks to how much of the city's economy now runs through platforms rather than visitor footfall. The UAE also has the Habshan-Fujairah pipeline to bypass Hormuz, a hedge the rest of the Gulf is now racing to copy. The war cost the UAE its oil GDP and its tourists, but finance and construction still grew, and Dubai's cashless collections hit 99.7%.

04Parts on credit
Saudi Autobia raises $12M Series A
Autobia, a Saudi platform connecting auto parts retailers with verified suppliers, raised a $12 million Series A led by Artal Capital, with Aljazira Capital and Wa'ed Ventures participating. Co-founded in 2021 by Emad Daghreri and Ahmed Alawfi, the company offers procurement, fulfilment, logistics, inventory financing and buy-now-pay-later.

The new money will go toward expansion across Saudi Arabia, logistics and fulfilment infrastructure, dynamic pricing technology and inventory financing. Autobia raised a $2.5 million seed in 2023 led by Sadu Capital with Wa'ed Ventures and Raz Holding participating.

Parts sellers in the Gulf stay cash-poor precisely because their inventory is expensive and their suppliers demand payment before shipment. A platform that underwrites both the stock and the credit is effectively a bank for the workshop floor, and the single largest auto market in the GCC is now backing that model with real capital.

05Italian towers
Mubadala eyes stake in Italian tower firm
Mubadala is in talks to take a stake in INWIT, Italy's largest mobile tower company, as part of a wider take-private of the business, according to reports. Ardian holds 32% and Oak Holdings, a KKR, Vodafone and GIP vehicle, holds 39%. INWIT posted revenue of EUR 1.1 billion and EBITDAaL of EUR 785 million last year, though its market value has dropped 27% over the past year.

Why a European tower play interests Abu Dhabi The Gulf's digital build-out is heavy on data centres and cloud, but passive infrastructure like towers is where European funds have generated steady, contracted cash flows. A take-private offers the same logic at a lower entry point than building from scratch, and Mubadala has been diversifying its telecom exposure through infrastructure for years. The bid is a clear sign that Abu Dhabi wants the unglamorous landlord of Europe's mobile networks, not just the flashy consumer brands. It is a cash-flow deal priced in a downturn, the kind of trade that becomes available only when others are selling.

The AI Race
​
Amazon chases Decart AI for $7B
Amazon is negotiating to acquire Decart AI for roughly $7 billion, according to reports. The startup raised over $400 million in venture capital and was valued at $4 billion in May, which means Amazon is paying a premium for whatever Decart has built in the last five months.

The move puts Amazon squarely against Microsoft, Google, and the Oracle-backed OpenAI, each of which is assembling its own vertically integrated AI stack. Decart's technology is said to focus on generative media and simulation, which would give Amazon a new layer between its cloud business and the consumer AI tools built by its rivals. It is a $7 billion bet that the next generation of AI won't just chat, but will generate whole worlds. The price also shows how fast AI valuations are climbing; a $4 billion valuation in May becoming a $7 billion deal by October is a 75% markup in less than half a year. Gulf sovereign funds hold positions across OpenAI, SoftBank, and AWS's hardware, so this deal deepens the same ecosystem they are already paying a premium to join.

Banque du Caire files to float 30% stake
Banque du Caire filed to float a 30% stake worth about EGP 24 billion, with Banque Misr offloading 4.575 billion shares. The bank earned EGP 8.9 billion in 1H 2026, up 22% year on year. It is one of the largest listings in a country that is still trying to rebuild its capital market after years of macro pressure. Beach Canteen moves to December, where it belongs
e& Beach Canteen is returning to Nessnass Beach in December after its original March slot was pushed back. The Last Sip
​
Which Gulf city is now 90 percent cashless, and what was the last 10 percent?
The answer
Dubai hit its 2026 target early at 90 percent cashless, with government collections at 99.7 percent. The last 10 percent is mostly small merchants, cross-border workers, and the informal economy. Regulators are already building the e-invoicing rails to catch exactly that segment. The practical effect is that every transaction now leaves a record, which changes how businesses, fintechs and tax authorities all behave. The end of cash is really the end of quietly paying the plumber in folding money.
Why is a 194-year-old tortoise scientifically interesting?
The answer
Jonathan, a 194-year-old Aldabra giant tortoise, has genetic traits tied to DNA repair and tumor suppression. Parts of his epigenome resemble a five-year-old tortoise. He has lived through two world wars and the invention of the smartphone. Scientists are studying him to understand why some species age so slowly. The tortoise's anti-aging secret is also the opposite of every self-help book ever written.
