The Lead

Hormuz tanker hit
Reportedly, four crew were injured when the Liberia-flagged supertanker MT Fujairah Energy was struck by a projectile on 10 November while transiting the Arabian Gulf. Six Indian seafarers were on board. One suffered minor injuries and was taken ashore. The remaining 18 crew, including five other Indian seafarers, were reported safe.

The UK Maritime Trade Operations reported a separate incident involving a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz early on 11 November. Crew status, extent of damage, and environmental impact were not immediately known. Adnoc Logistics and Services confirmed one of its own vessels sustained damage following an attack in UAE territorial waters on Tuesday afternoon, 10 November. The company did not disclose the vessel identity or the extent of the damage. Reportedly, an Adnoc-affiliated vessel was previously struck by Iranian missiles in August. Two of its crude oil tankers were hit in the Strait of Hormuz in July, killing one crew member. The attacks come as oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz averaged 7.82 million barrels per day over the week to 8 November. That is about 46% of the prewar level of 17.14 million barrels per day, per Kpler. Brent was trading at $92.42 a barrel on Wednesday. Every hull in the Gulf now carries a war-risk premium that no insurer has figured out how to price. Shipping costs and insurance premiums for Gulf-bound cargo will rise further. Every operator moving goods through Hormuz must now factor in delays, rerouting, and the real possibility of losing a vessel, reportedly.

02PIF's funding pivot
PIF explores securitization to raise funds
The Public Investment Fund is reportedly exploring securitizing future income from its portfolio companies to raise funds. No transactions, companies, or amounts have been identified. The fund secured $3 billion in financing backed by Italy's export credit agency SACE earlier this month.

PIF sold 49% of AI company Humain to Aramco, which will put SAR 9.9 billion into the business this year. It also completed a 70% stake sale in Al Hilal to Kingdom Holding for SAR 840 million in September. The fund is clearly moving from holding assets to harvesting them.

The funding push comes as the Kingdom's budget faces oil prices that have not recovered to the levels it needs. It also comes as LIV Golf, a PIF-backed asset, filed for Chapter 11 this week. Securitizing future income is the Gulf equivalent of selling tomorrow's harvest to buy today's seeds. The question is what gets planted next. For investors and operators watching PIF, the signal is that the fund is now managing its balance sheet like a private equity firm rather than a sovereign vault.
Source: Reuters and Associated Press

03300 milliseconds, inshallah
Wafr raises angel round to digitize sukuk
UAE fintech Wafr raised an undisclosed pre-seed round from angels Coen Jonker and Neal Cross to digitize sukuk issuance and settlement. The company launched WafrChain, a permissioned blockchain that reaches transaction finality in 300 milliseconds and can operate with only a third of its nodes active.

The pitch is simple: global Islamic finance assets reached $6.5 trillion in 2025, and annual sukuk issuance rose 14.5% to $291.4 billion. Wafr wants a slice of the settlement layer.

That is an enormous market for a startup whose entire staff could likely fit in one Dubai conference room. The $1.14 trillion in outstanding sukuk still settles through slow, manual rails, and Wafr is betting that 300 milliseconds beats three days. The room may be small, but the table is big. Angels Jonker and Cross have backed a team that fits in one Dubai conference room to digitize the rails under $1.14 trillion in sukuk.

04Paris on tap
Veolia wins $225m Gulf desalination deals
Veolia secured $225 million in desalination deals across Oman and the UAE. The French firm will operate the Al-Asilah plant in Oman and provide design and technology services for Sharjah's Al-Hamriyah project.

The wins extend Veolia's already-deep footprint in a region where desalination is not a choice but the only tap. Abu Dhabi still gets 70% of its farm water from groundwater, while desalination supplies just 18%, a number every new contract quietly pushes higher.

Water security is the Gulf's other strait, and French engineering keeps winning the contracts to build it. For local operators, that means the skills and systems increasingly arrive with a Paris accent. The only tap is being fitted with another $225 million worth of pipe.

05Waller's half-point
US inflation seals December Fed hike
US consumer price inflation accelerated 0.4% monthly in October, above estimates and up from an already-hot September. Core inflation rose 0.3% month on month, also above forecasts. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed back to 5%.

Around 90% of traders expect the Fed to raise rates 25 basis points in December, per CME Group data. About 10% are betting on 50 basis points. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he would support a 50-basis-point hike if the October inflation held firm. The rate path matters directly to Gulf borrowers, because Gulf central banks peg to the dollar and import the Fed's decisions wholesale. A stronger dollar and higher US rates mean tighter liquidity in Riyadh, Abu Dhabi, and Doha. This is true even if their own economies are absorbing oil money just fine.

Maritime insurance and ship-to-ship oil transfers driven by the Iran war are reportedly adding $20 to $30 per barrel to oil prices, per Waller. That keeps a floor under Gulf revenues even as it feeds the very inflation that forces the Fed to stay tight. The region is on both sides of the same trade. Gulf corporates should price in another US rate hike next month. That means dollar funding will get more expensive even as oil money cushions sovereign balance sheets.
Source: The National Business

The AI Race
​
Saudi law opens the AI training door
Saudi Arabia's new copyright law, effective August, explicitly allows using copyrighted materials for AI training. The law joins Japan, the EU, and Singapore in permitting the practice. It requires developers to obtain works lawfully, use them only for model training, and log type, source, purpose, and date of each use.

The implementing regulations allow Saudi entities to translate works into Arabic without the copyright holder's consent if no Arabic translation appears within three years. That is a direct structural advantage for Arabic-language model builders inside the Kingdom. The UAE's Jais model was trained on servers in California, so it falls under US copyright protections. UAE law currently has no AI training carveout or fair use doctrine. Matthew Sag, a law professor at Emory University, predicts the UAE will likely update its own copyright regime within 18 months. Saudi developers can now build on copyrighted Arabic text, image, and audio corpora without the legal exposure their UAE counterparts carry, and that gap will shape where the next Arabic foundation models get built.
