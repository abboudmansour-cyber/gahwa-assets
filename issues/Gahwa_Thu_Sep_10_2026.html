The Lead
​
Tanker for tanker: the Hormuz war just became a shipping insurance crisis with a Saudi price tag.
Saudi Arabia is stepping in to insure ships against Hormuz war risk, with Saudi Re arranging the cover. The Saudi Pool for Marine Insurance will cover cargo, hull, charterers' liability, and protection and indemnity, under the supervision of the Insurance Authority. The state is stepping in because private insurers have repriced the risk of Hormuz beyond what many shippers can bear.

War-risk premiums for vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz have risen to between 3% and 10% of a vessel's value, up from about 0.25% before the conflict. That means insuring a single large tanker can now cost between $3 million and $10 million. The figures come from Marsh data cited by Asharq Business. The strait normally carries roughly one-fifth of global oil and LNG supplies.

The cover is capped at up to SAR 700 million in commercial cover per insured event. That is a meaningful backstop, but it is not a blank cheque. The pool is designed to keep Saudi cargo moving, not to subsidise the whole Gulf. Finance Minister Mohammed Aljadaan cited the initiative as part of the kingdom's response to the conflict.

The wider picture is grim. The US reportedly destroyed five Iranian oil tankers on Wednesday, and Iran claimed attacks on 10 ships near the strait. A seafarer was killed, officials say. Brent crude settled above $100 a barrel for the first time since late May, and preliminary Kpler data showed only six commodity vessels passed through the strait on Tuesday compared with a ten-day average of twelve, according to Kpler. Pre-war, about 125 commercial vessels crossed daily. Saudi Arabia has chosen to protect its trade with a financial instrument rather than a convoy. The pool is the clearest signal yet that the kingdom expects the Hormuz standoff to last. For Gulf shippers, the insurance pool changes the near-term maths: cargo can move under a Saudi backstop, but the premium shock will still land on every invoice that passes through Hormuz.

02PIF on Wall Street
PIF courts six Wall Street giants
Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund is courting six of the biggest alternative asset managers on Wall Street: Apollo, Blackstone, Brookfield, Carlyle, KKR, and Stonepeak. The meetings are taking place in New York, according to Semafor. No specific transaction is attached to the talks yet.

The fund's portfolio companies are part of the pitch. Humain, King Abdullah Financial District, and Red Sea Global are participating in the roadshow. The message is that PIF has assets beyond oil and beyond the headlines, and it wants long-term partners, not just cheque writers.

The numbers back the pitch. PIF's May bond raised $7 billion against a $29 billion order book. Its Saudi Real Estate Refinance Company pulled $16.75 billion of demand for a $2.75 billion sukuk in July. The fund is also hunting a new lead investor for LIV Golf, targeting $250 million to $350 million after the golf league filed for bankruptcy.

Why Wall Street now? The regional war has made Gulf capital more valuable, not less. Global investors are looking for large, stable pools of money that can deploy quickly. Saudi Arabia wants that capital to flow into its domestic projects, not just its sovereign bonds. The roadshow is a test of whether PIF can convert bond-market demand into equity partnerships. For Gulf operators, the takeaway is simple: when PIF brings in six of the world's largest investors, it is not just raising money, it is setting the price for Saudi assets.

03Mal's global waitlist
Mal opens global waitlist after $230m
A $230 million seed-backed Abu Dhabi fintech opened a global waitlist three months after receiving In-Principle Approval from the UAE central bank. The company raised $230 million in seed funding, which it says is the largest publicly announced fintech seed investment in the Middle East and Africa. Mal was founded in 2025 by Emirati entrepreneur Abdallah Abu-Sheikh.

Mal is an AI-native Islamic financial platform covering payments, money transfers, financing, and investment. Its products include global accounts for freelancers, AI-powered personal finance, lifestyle financing, and business solutions for SMEs. The waitlist offers priority access and a joining bonus, with referrals unlocking a Mal Club tier that includes transfer fee savings, cashback, and travel offers.

The company plans a simultaneous multi-market launch, using the UAE licence as a base to enter other jurisdictions. That is ambitious for a startup that is only a year old. The $230 million seed gives it a runway most fintechs can only envy.

Mal is betting that Islamic finance can be delivered through AI-native digital rails. If it works, it could redraw the regional fintech map. The central bank approval is the critical piece: it means Mal can operate as a licensed financial institution rather than a technology partner. For Gulf fintech founders, Mal's round resets the ceiling: a $230 million seed for an Islamic finance platform means the region's cheque sizes have entered a new league.

04Ades rigs return
Ades restarts 10 rigs after war
Ades Holding is bringing 10 rigs back online this month after the regional conflict cost it roughly $30 million in foregone revenue every month for a quarter. The Saudi drilling company reported a 31.9% year-on-year drop in second-quarter net income. Its contract backlog stands at about SAR 34 billion, excluding a $285 million acquisition of Saipem rigs.

The restart is a sign that some Gulf operators are learning to work around the conflict rather than waiting for it to end. The Saipem rig acquisition is expected to add $50 million to next year's EBITDA. The Shelf Drilling acquisition contributes another $400 million. A Syria gas production contract worth about $20 million annually comes online in the second half of 2026.

Insurance costs have already fallen about 75% from their peak, another sign of adaptation. Ades expects SAR 17 billion of its backlog to convert to liquidity over five years. The company is effectively rebuilding its revenue base while the rest of the region reprices risk.

The drilling sector is a bellwether for Gulf energy services. If Ades can bring rigs back and restore revenue while the war continues, other operators will follow. The question is whether the insurance market stays calm enough to make that bet pay. Ades is not waiting for peace. It is pricing the war and getting back to work, which is the most useful signal a Gulf operator can borrow.

UAE-Germany billions
UAE-Germany billions incoming
The UAE and Germany are set to announce agreements worth billions of dollars spanning investment, AI, technology, and energy. The announcement is timed around a state visit. The existing bilateral relationship is worth about $15.5 billion, excluding oil.

The deals signal a deepening beyond energy. The UAE has been leaning into AI and technology partnerships with European countries. Germany, for its part, wants access to Gulf capital and a reliable energy partner.

The timing matters. Europe is looking for stable sources of investment as its own growth slows. The UAE is looking for technology and industrial know-how. The two economies fit together, and the announcement is likely to be a series of specific projects rather than a vague memorandum. The agreements will create direct procurement and partnership opportunities for UAE firms in AI and energy, especially those already working with German suppliers.

The AI Race
​
Abu Dhabi's $1bn space bet
Abu Dhabi is scaling Altair, a 50-satellite AI-enabled constellation, into a $1 billion infrastructure play. Marlan Space and Loft Orbital lead the consortium, backed by France's Mistral and US-based BlackSky. The first ten satellites are already in production at a Kezad facility in Abu Dhabi. The first satellite launches in October.

Mistral will deploy AI models alongside the satellite sensors while BlackSky provides high-resolution optical imaging. France's MaiaSpace is the preferred launch partner. IHC, the Abu Dhabi conglomerate, agreed to acquire an 80% stake in Marlan through its International Tech Group. That gives the project a deep-pocketed local owner and ties the Gulf to two of the West's most capable space and AI ecosystems. It is a move from renting AI infrastructure to owning it. A Gulf city is testing whether it can own the infrastructure layer of the AI economy, not just rent space on someone else's.
Business
​
Saudi contracting awards halve in August
The value of Saudi construction contracts dropped to SAR 4.86 billion in August from SAR 9.75 billion in July. The number of projects rose to 13 from 11, but the average size shrank sharply. Year on year, the value was flat against August 2025.
FAB's $500m bond priced tight
First Abu Dhabi Bank priced a five year senior unsecured bond at 5.456 percent. The deal was more than two times oversubscribed. Pricing tightened 25 basis points from initial guidance.
MAF posts record half but retail slips
Majid Al Futtaim reported record EBITDA of AED 2.5 billion in the first half, up 11 percent. Net operating income after tax rose 25 percent to AED 1.8 billion. Net income fell 24.7 percent to AED 1.1 billion as retail revenue slipped 6 percent.
Azizi enters Sharjah with AED 30bn project
Azizi Developments announced Azizi Florence, a AED 30 billion Sharjah project and its first in the emirate. It includes 1,100 villas, over 6,000 townhouses and 3,500 apartments. It is the fourth UAE mega-project worth AED 30 billion or more since May.
Dow weighs exiting $20bn Sadara JV
Dow Inc. is considering a sale of its 35 percent stake in Sadara Chemical, the $20 billion joint venture with Aramco. Dow also holds a guarantee on a $500 million revolving credit facility for the venture. Sadara drew $80 million from that facility in the fourth quarter of 2025.
Alpha Wave joins $1bn Adani Airports raise
Alpha Wave Global, part of Abu Dhabi's IHC, is joining a roughly $1 billion capital raise for Adani Airport Holdings. Premji Invest, Temasek and BlackRock managed funds are also in the round. Adani has a INR 400 billion, or $4.8 billion, capex plan through fiscal 2027.

The Last Sip
​
Which Gulf city has a bakery now rising in Manhattan?
The answer
Manama, by way of Dona Murad. She opened Hopscotch Bakery and Café and Grind Micro Roastery there in 2015. In 2022 she co-founded Librae Bakery in New York City with her husband. Now a Bahraini roastery and a New York bakery carry the same name out of the same small kingdom. The fastest crossover out of Bahrain is no longer a gas contract, it is a croissant.
What runs at speeds from $3 million to $10 million and cannot be caught?
The answer
A single supertanker trying to pass through the Strait of Hormuz. That is the war-risk premium now charged per trip, up from around 0.25 percent of a vessel's value. Private insurers reset the price and Saudi Arabia answered with a state pool. The navy is for headlines. The insurance pool is for the delivery date.
Where do ten satellites fit in a Kezad facility?
The answer
In Abu Dhabi, on a production line built to scale from ten to fifty. The first ten Altair-Next Gen satellites are already being assembled there. The first launch is slated for October. The UAE is building a shelf and the satellites go on it like cartons of dates.
