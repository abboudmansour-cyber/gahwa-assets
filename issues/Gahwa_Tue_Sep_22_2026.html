Domestic Trips Prop Up Saudi Tourism As Foreign Arrivals Drop
Saudi Arabia's inbound tourist arrivals fell 13% year-on-year to 8.3 million in the first quarter of 2026. The decline came as the regional conflict deterred high-spending foreign visitors, with international visitor spending down 7% to SAR 48 billion.

Domestic trips rose 16% to 28.9 million, pushing total tourists up 8% to 37.2 million despite the drop in foreign arrivals. But a domestic tourist spent about SAR 1,200 in the quarter, while an international tourist spent roughly SAR 5,800. Total tourism spending still fell 2% to SAR 82.7 billion, showing that locals cannot replace foreign wallets one-for-one.

The split runs through every layer of the sector. Makkah occupancy rose 3.1 percentage points and average daily rates climbed 2.1%, while Riyadh occupancy fell 10.8 points and revenue per available room dropped 26%. Licensed hospitality facilities reached 6,122 in Q1, up 22.7% year-on-year, and the Kingdom still has 200,000 hotel rooms under development through 2030 backed by over $120 billion in investment. The question now is whether that supply can wait for foreign demand to return, or whether operators must reprice for a domestic-only market for the rest of the war. The domestic pivot is real but smaller: every domestic trip adds about a fifth of what a foreign visitor spends, so operators face a math problem, not just a marketing one.

02UAE fuel retail
Adnoc Eyes $1bn Egypt Fuel Retail Push
Adnoc Distribution is studying an investment of about $1 billion to add roughly 400 service stations to the 245 it already operates in Egypt, a move that would make it the largest private fuel retailer in the country. The investment would likely run in two phases of about 200 stations each, and it includes automotive and engine lubricants. Adnoc is considering a partnership with the Egyptian General Petroleum Corporation, or it may build independently or lease stations.

The Egyptian push follows Adnoc's July agreement worth an implied $1 billion to take over Shell South Africa's downstream business, which is set to close in 2027 and adds 55% to Adnoc's global network, bringing it to nearly 1,600 service stations. Egypt already accounts for about 36% of Adnoc Distribution's aviation-fuel sales volumes, with 129 million liters of 357 million liters in the first half of 2026, though Egyptian aviation fuel volume grew only 3.9% year-on-year against the group's 53.9%. Aviation generates more than 60% of Adnoc Distribution's Egypt-based EBITDA.

Adnoc Distribution is also preparing to start aircraft refueling at Sphinx Airport in October and Hurghada by year-end as part of a $50 million push under its EGPC agreement. The broader pattern is clear: Adnoc sees Egypt as the volume answer to its regional downstream ambitions, and the Shell South Africa. Adnoc's Egyptian expansion turns a slow-growth aviation fuel business into a race for retail volume, and the scale of the bet raises the stakes for every fuel retailer in North Africa.

03SME debt push
Lendo, Quantic Launch $200m SME Financing Programme
Saudi fintech Lendo has partnered with Austria-based Quantic Financial Solutions to launch an institutional capital programme of up to SAR 750 million, or about $200 million, to finance small and medium enterprises in Saudi Arabia. The deal was announced at the Money20/20 Middle East conference in Riyadh. Quantic will structure shariah-compliant investment products based on the financed assets for international investors.

Lendo operates a debt crowdfunding platform regulated by the Saudi Central Bank, and it has already financed almost SAR 5 billion in development. The partnership plugs into the broader push by Saudi policymakers to close the SME credit gap, a key plank of the Financial Sector Development Program under Vision 2030. Institutional capital coming in from Austria is a signal that Gulf SME debt is now investible for global funds, not just local banks.

The scale of the programme, at $200 million, is large enough to matter for the many Saudi SMEs that cannot access bank credit but have real invoices and assets. The challenge will be converting Austrian institutional appetite into on-the-ground underwriting that stays true to these loans' performance, rather than just their structure. A Vienna-based asset manager now believes Gulf small business debt is worth $200 million of structuring, and that is a louder signal for Saudi SMEs than any government policy paper.

04Bypass with a consultant
Hormuz Workarounds Hit $1bn Consultancy Stage
Saudi Arabia and the UAE are in early-stage talks with India's Engineers India Limited for consultancy and engineering mandates on about $1 billion in pipelines, storage, and export terminals designed to reduce reliance on the Strait of Hormuz. Fujairah already accounts for roughly 66% of the UAE's total oil exports, and DP World is building a new multipurpose port and two terminals along the Fujairah coastline.

According to officials, the broader push involves redundant export routes that can keep crude and products moving if Hormuz is blocked or made too dangerous. Aramco has doubled daily loadings from Ras Tanura and Juaymah to two very large crude carriers, about 4 million barrels, and sold roughly 60 million barrels from Ras Tanura for ship-to-ship transfer off Sohar, Oman. These are stopgaps, but they are becoming permanent infrastructure bets.

The consultancy stage matters because it locks in technical specifications and route priorities before the heavy construction money follows. Indian engineering firms now sit inside the Gulf's most strategic energy logistics planning, and that is a shift from the era when these decisions stayed within national oil companies and a small circle of Western contractors. For Gulf shippers and terminal operators, the pipeline and storage build-out is now a multi-year revenue stream, not a crisis response. Fujairah already ships two-thirds of the UAE's crude, so the pipeline talk is about the other third.

05Twelve easy payments
Rize Locks $50m Murabaha Facility From Jadwa
Saudi proptech Rize has secured a SAR 187.5 million asset-backed Murabaha facility from Jadwa Investment, about $50 million, to finance its portfolio of residential rental contracts. Rize runs a rent-now-pay-later model that splits annual rent into 12 monthly payments, and it claims more than 200,000 tenants have used its flexible rental solutions. The company says its network includes over 3,000 landlords and 1,200 verified real estate brokers.

The facility is a structured Islamic finance deal, using the rental contracts as the underlying assets, which is a meaningful validation for proptech in a market where residential rent and property management have been slow to digitise. Rize previously raised a $35 million Series A in January 2025, and Saudi Arabia's residential rental market is estimated at around SAR 150 billion annually. The company is licensed by the Real Estate General Authority for electronic brokerage and property management.

The significance is that institutional Islamic capital, not just venture equity, is now underwriting the rent-now-pay-later model. If the Murabaha structure performs, it creates a template for other fintechs and proptechs to tap Jadwa-style capital, and that would deepen the debt market for Saudi consumer finance far beyond what venture funding alone could do. Jadwa is now financing rent payments the way banks finance car leases, and that turns a startup model into an asset class.

The AI Race
​
World Model Builders Go Quiet
Companies building world models, AI systems that simulate real environments, are sitting on large cash reserves while disclosing almost nothing about their work, per TechCrunch. Founders and their data suppliers are reluctant to reveal what they are building, a shift from the loud funding announcements that marked the field's first wave.

The silence reflects intense competition in frontier AI. When cash is abundant and staffing wars are fierce, the only edge left is secrecy around training data, model architecture, and product timing. World models matter because they underpin robotics, autonomous vehicles, and industrial simulation, all areas where the Gulf is now buying systems rather than building them. Gulf sovereigns are signing AI deals with Abu Dhabi and Seoul, but the world-model layer is going dark, which means procurement teams will be buying capability they cannot audit.

QIA Launches Doha Investment Division
Qatar's sovereign wealth fund QIA announced 'Doha Investment,' a new domestic investment division, at the Qatar Economic Forum in New York. The Prime Minister said Qatar expects to award $38.5 billion in infrastructure projects over five years. The state also unveiled a $60 billion investment pipeline. The new division signals that domestic deployment is now a formal mandate, not a side project.
Sharjah Islamic Bank Prepares USD Sukuk
Sharjah Islamic Bank is planning a benchmark-sized USD sukuk, appointing 12 joint lead managers including Mashreq, DIB, and Standard Chartered. Its November 2025 sukuk raised USD 500 million at a 4.60% yield with USD 1.35 billion in orders. FAB and Mashreq each priced USD 500 million bonds last week. The wave of Gulf bank issuance shows lenders are locking in dollar funding while the war premium is still priced in.
Middle East Q2 Dividends Hit USD 44.6bn
Middle East companies paid USD 44.6 billion in dividends in Q2 2026, up 5.5% year-on-year, per Janus Henderson. Saudi Arabia paid USD 28.4 billion, the UAE USD 13.6 billion, and Kuwait USD 1.8 billion. Kuwait was the fastest-growing at 45%. The numbers show Gulf payouts are still rising even as war, travel warnings, and pipeline attacks cloud the operating environment.
Nadec takes full control of Al Raie National Livestock
Saudi dairy firm Nadec bought the 49% stake held by Anaam Saudi for Trading for SAR 23.69 million. The deal, agreed in May, gives Nadec 100% ownership of Al Raie National Livestock. It consolidates Nadec's position in domestic livestock.
Dubai prepares tenders for Jebel Ali sewage PPP
Dubai is preparing tenders for sewage treatment plants, including the Jebel Ali public-private partnership that will add up to 1 million cubic metres a day of capacity. The project supports Dubai's urban expansion and sustainability goals.

Off Paper

Qatar's Stamp Collectors Win Medals
The Qatar Philatelic and Numismatic Center won medals at the Egyphila Stamp Exhibition, a quiet nod to a hobby with deep regional roots. The center's members collect, preserve, and exhibit stamps and coins that trace Gulf history. At a time when everything moves at screen speed, a stamp album is a small act of patience. Heritage institutions like this keep the region's trade and postal story alive for the next generation.

Hotels Are Building Bee Havens
Hotels across the Gulf and beyond are carving out dedicated bee habitats on their properties and making them part of the guest experience. The trend turns a sustainability footnote into a visible amenity, with hives on rooftops and pollinator gardens near pools. It is a small, concrete way for hospitality to answer demand for greener stays. In a market where every beach club and hotel fights for the same resident's weekend, a working beehive is a genuine differentiator.
The Last Sip
​
Which Gulf city is building a stadium that took four years just to get twenty contractors interested?
The answer
Manama. Bahrain's Sports City stadium at Sakhir is part of the $30 billion Strategic Projects Plan announced in 2021. Twenty contractors have now prequalified to bid, but the project still has not broken ground. In Gulf infrastructure years, a stadium that is still a list of bidders after four years is either very thorough or very slow. The most competitive sport in Bahrain right now is the tender process.
Why are European refiners suddenly scrambling for non-Saudi crude?
The answer
Aramco told European refining customers they will receive no crude next month under long-term contracts. The cut follows drone strikes on Saudi pipelines and comes as the East-West pipeline runs at reduced capacity. Since Europe cannot quickly replace contracted Saudi volumes, buyers must chase spot cargoes that now carry a war premium. The cut is official; the scramble is just beginning.
