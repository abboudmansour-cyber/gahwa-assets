The Lead
​
Oil tops $110 as Hormuz worries mount
Brent settled at $109.86 a barrel on Friday, up 3.72% on the day and the highest close since mid-May.

West Texas Intermediate rose 3.55% to $101.41, its first close above $100 in nearly a month.

The engine is Iran's campaign against tankers and cargo ships in the Strait of Hormuz. Over the past three weeks, it has moved from a simmering threat to a systemic bottleneck.

Analysts say the immediate trigger was a fresh spike in attacks over the past 48 hours. Multiple vessels were struck, one lost, and several disabled or not under command.

The IEA responded by agreeing to release more than 20 million barrels to market. But traders have so far treated this as a short-term fix rather than a solution to the underlying problem.

Diesel is the sharper pain. Wholesale diesel surpassed $200 in Singapore and hit roughly $220 in Europe and the US, and the US average pump price reached $6.32 a gallon. The IEA has already warned the global diesel shortage will persist well into 2027.

In this climate, every Gulf shipper and importer now confronts a structural problem. The fastest way to move oil out of the region runs through a strait that is effectively a war zone. That is not a spike. It is a new cost base.

02The maturity wall
GCC bond wall is $491bn
GCC issuers carry roughly $491 billion in bonds and sukuk maturing through 2030, according to Bloomberg data compiled by Kamco Invest. The heaviest year is 2029 at $143 billion.

The UAE alone has $46 billion due that year. Across the region, more than 725 corporate bonds worth about $117 billion mature between 2027 and 2030, and $99.4 billion of those are investment grade.

The US 10-year Treasury, the foundation for global borrowing costs, hit a 24-year intraday high of 5.35% on October 8. For finance directors across the region, the arithmetic is unforgiving: a bond or sukuk that came due cheaply three years ago will now have to be refinanced at rates more than double, and the window to do anything about it is closing, according to the newsletter's analysis.
Source: Bloomberg and Financial Times

03100MW in the capital
Riyadh tenders $1bn AI data centre
Humain has put $1 billion of Al-Saad AI data centre work out to tender. The tender covers the engineering, procurement and construction of its Al-Saad AI data centre in Riyadh, a project estimated at $1 billion.

The initial capacity is 100 megawatts, with an option to expand to 200MW. That would make it one of the largest AI-ready data centres in the Kingdom outside the megaprojects.

The timing is deliberate. With both Neom and Qiddiya still scaling, Riyadh itself is now the more immediate market for AI compute, and a 100MW build inside the capital is a signal that private operators are moving faster than the giga-project timelines.

Contractors across the Gulf will read this tender as the start of the next wave of Saudi data centre procurement, and the ones who missed the first wave are already on the back foot.
Source: MEED Saudi Arabia

04Air quotes
Tesla's flying Roadster is 2028 reality
Ten cold-gas thrusters and 600 liters of compressed air let a redesigned Roadster briefly drive upside down on a parabolic ramp. It also lets the car leap over five parked Teslas.

The performance numbers are theatrical: 0-100 kph in around one second, a top speed above 230 mph, and an air tank that self-recharges without propellant. But the flying version is a limited edition only. No street-legal thruster version is planned.

Musk himself said, 'I'm not sure if it's necessarily legal in all jurisdictions.' That is as close as a carmaker gets to admitting a feature is for publicity, not parking. Three trims are planned, with waitlist deposits of $50,000 for the track-ready Thrust and $250,000 for the invite-only Rocket.

Production is slated for 2028, which is Tesla shorthand. For the Gulf driver wondering if this is the car for Sheikh Zayed Road: it is not. It is a very expensive, very fast circus act that happens to have wheels.

The AI Race
​
Meta’s Mac escape
Meta released Muse Spark Open, an 816-billion-parameter open-weight model. It has 42 billion active parameters per request and a 1-million-token context window. The striking detail is not the size. It runs locally on two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios, about $40,000 in hardware, with no cloud dependency. Apple updated its MLX toolkit to make the pairing smooth. That is a notable thaw after years of App Store friction with Meta.

The model scores 39 on Artificial Analysis’ Intelligence Index. That is behind China’s GLM 5.3 at 45 and Kimi K3 at 44. But it is the most capable Western open-weight model to run entirely on local hardware. That matters more than benchmark points for regulated industries. It removes the cloud as a middleman for anyone who wants to run a frontier model in-house. The two Mac Studios cost less than the compliance meetings about whether to use them.

McDermott cuts steel on Adnoc’s $500m Sarb gas
McDermott has begun fabrication on Adnoc’s Sarb gas project contract, awarded in January. The package is worth an estimated $500 million. It covers engineering, procurement and construction for a facility that will process around 700 million cubic feet per day. The project is a substantial part of Adnoc’s push to expand gas output.
Trump waives Russia sanctions and tariffs on energy before they take effect
President Trump signed an executive order on Friday waiving the sweeping penalties tied to Russia's energy revenues that were set to hit this week. The move lets Russia supply millions of barrels of diesel to global markets, a direct trade for relief from US sanctions that Ukrainian President Zelensky called a 'weak decision'.
CoreWeave shares slide again as debt stress deepens
CoreWeave fell 11.2% to $61.78 on Friday, down 21% over five trading days. The company carries about $52 billion in debt against a $35 billion market cap, and the cost of insuring its debt against default has priced a 60% chance of insolvency within five years.
Riyadh readies its first fully autonomous metro line
The line is expected to carry 100,000 passengers daily when it opens next month, and will run entirely driverless. The operator says it will run at two-minute intervals during peak hours, a frequency no Gulf metro yet matches.
