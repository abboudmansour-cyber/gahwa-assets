Hormuz-Proof Refinery Narrows Host to Three GCC Sites
The planned 200,000-barrel-per-day facility is, according to project backers, a direct structural answer to the Strait of Hormuz closure that has scrambled Gulf energy logistics since late February. With Iranian forces effectively blocking the waterway, Adnoc has resorted to running LNG carriers dark through the strait with transponders off, Fertiglobe is trucking fertilizer overland and negotiating with Etihad Rail, and oil spills from vessels stranded for months are now fouling Oman's Musandam beaches. A refinery purpose-built outside the chokepoint turns a costly vulnerability into a permanent competitive moat.

The geopolitical frame sharpens the business case. Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman is in Washington lobbying for F-35 fighter jets, and Riyadh has just assembled a 14-nation maritime alliance to secure the Bab al-Mandab Strait. A new export complex anchored on a safer coastline is not merely a supply-chain fix; it is a declaration that the Gulf's energy map is being redrawn for good. The three unnamed GCC states still in the running are competing for a piece of infrastructure that promises to be as much a strategic asset as a commercial one.
Host selection is expected by the end of the year, and the decision will shape the Gulf's energy export map for decades.

UAE Bond Sales Hit $30.3 Billion Record
Sovereigns and corporates sold USD 30.3 billion of USD- and EUR-denominated bonds through July 28, up one-third year-on-year and USD 3.7 billion above the previous record set six years ago.

The surge spans the credit spectrum. Mashreq placed a USD 500 million AT1, Rakbank issued USD 600 million, Burjeel Holdings sold a USD 500 million five-year sukuk at 7.125 percent, and Ajman Bank tapped the market for a dollar-denominated AT1 sukuk. Abu Dhabi raised a further USD 2.5 billion through private placements, while FAB and ENDB also accessed private markets. The reclassification of UAE debt to developed-market status in JPMorgan's emerging-market bond index triggered a four-month phased selloff that pushed sovereign spreads to around 77 basis points over US Treasuries, yet borrowing costs remain broadly unchanged from pre-war levels.

Issuers are frontloading liquidity. With the Fed holding rates at 3.5 to 3.75 percent and three regional presidents dissenting in favor of a hike, the window for cheap funding may not stay open long, and Gulf borrowers are filling their boots while it lasts. Issuers are likely to continue frontloading liquidity into the second half of 2026 before any rate hike narrows the window.

Saudi Arabia H1 Deficit Widens to $43 Billion
Government revenue rose 6 percent year-on-year to USD 160.7 billion, but that was no match for expenditures that climbed 15 percent to USD 203.6 billion, pushing the first-half deficit to SAR 160 billion.

Oil revenue increased 9 percent to USD 88.4 billion, while non-oil revenue edged up just 2 percent to USD 72.3 billion. The real pressure sits on the spending side: compensation of employees rose 3 percent to USD 78.9 billion, goods and services spending jumped 25 percent to USD 46.2 billion, and capital expenditure surged 32 percent to USD 24 billion. Financing expenses rose 20 percent to USD 7.8 billion as public debt climbed to USD 451.5 billion, up from USD 407.1 billion at the start of the year.

The deficit was financed entirely through borrowing, a pattern the market has grown comfortable with but that adds weight to a debt load already 44 billion dollars heavier than January. The second quarter alone added a USD 9.2 billion shortfall, and with Vision 2030 capex commitments running hot and oil prices pinned around USD 88, the second half will test whether the spending pace is sustainable or merely ambitious. The Q2 deficit alone was USD 9.2 billion, and with capex up 32 percent, the full-year trajectory will be closely watched by bond investors.

Trump Announces Hamas Disarmament Deal
The agreement, which Trump cast as a major milestone in his 20-Point Plan, is reportedly structured in phases with Israeli forces withdrawing as disarmament proceeds and an International Stabilization Force working alongside a new Palestinian police force.

Senior Hamas officials confirmed the deal to AFP, saying it includes provisions on weapons and a gradual Israeli withdrawal from the Gaza Strip. Trump thanked mediators Egypt, Qatar, and Turkey, and the announcement arrives nine months after a US-brokered ceasefire that had largely failed to hold.

The disarmament pledge is the sharpest articulation yet of a post-war governance framework for Gaza, but the phased structure leaves ample room for breakdown. The deal's durability will depend on whether the new Palestinian police force can establish credibility faster than the old grievances can resurface. If the phased disarmament holds, it opens the door to a new Palestinian governance structure in Gaza and could reduce the security premium Gulf states have priced into their budgets.

Saudi Startup Funding Drops 74 Percent in First Half
Saudi startups raised USD 219 million across 72 rounds in the first half of 2026, a 74 percent year-on-year decline against a 41 percent drop in round count. No deal topped USD 100 million, and pre-seed and seed funding fell 48 percent, while Series B and C rounds vanished entirely.

Fintech absorbed 67 percent of all Saudi startup funding despite a 41 percent decline in the sector itself. Saudi-based investors supplied 74 percent of capital, up from 54 percent a year earlier, while the international share collapsed to 13 percent from 36 percent. Merak Capital was the most active investor, deploying USD 7.1 million across 19 transactions. The retreat of foreign capital and the absence of large rounds suggest the ecosystem is consolidating around a narrower set of domestic backers writing smaller cheques, a correction that mirrors the global VC reset but hits harder in a market where international validation has long been treated as a leading indicator. Early-stage founders should expect longer fundraising cycles and valuations anchored to domestic rather than international benchmarks for the rest of 2026.

CULTURE
Art Basel Qatar Debuts With a Sharper Regional Lens
The inaugural Art Basel Qatar launched in Doha with 87 exhibitors and a format intentionally different from the fair's established Swiss and Hong Kong editions. Half the featured artists came from the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia, and the single-artist presentation model gave each booth the focus of a small museum room.

Sales data suggests the market was receptive. Ahmed Mater sold 11 works at prices between USD 45,000 and USD 220,000 at ATHR Gallery, eight Georg Baselitz bronze hand sculptures moved at 800,000 euros each at White Cube, and at least one Philip Guston painting priced up to USD 14 million found a buyer at Hauser & Wirth. The fair was produced in partnership with Qatar Sports Investments and QC+, the commercial arm of Qatar Museums, and it sits inside a broader cultural calendar that now includes the Qatar National Pavilion at the Venice Biennale and an upcoming major retrospective on the Baghdad Modern Art Group.

The message is deliberate: Qatar is building cultural infrastructure at the same tempo it builds ports and pipelines. A USD 14 million painting is not a substitute for a refinery, but it does signal which audience a country is courting. The fair's commercial success and regional focus establish Doha as a credible third pole in the art-world calendar alongside Basel and Hong Kong, with implications for hospitality, real estate, and luxury retail.
