The Lead
Hormuz Still Moving, Barely
​
US Central Command says it has helped roughly 1,300 commercial ships through the Strait of Hormuz since early May, moving more than 660 million barrels of crude. That works out to about seven million barrels per day over the past three weeks. The number matters because it is still far below the 20 million barrels a day that used to flow before the war. The US Navy is escorting vessels through the world's most important oil chokepoint while enforcing a blockade on Iranian ports, a strange dual role that makes every transit an act of naval choreography, according to officials.

The private trackers tell a different story. Windward and Kpler put crude exports at about 1.6 million barrels per day in May, rising to 4 million in June and 5 million in July. That is a recovery, but not a full one. At least some ships have still been hit despite the escorts, a reminder that no navy can guarantee a strait that is only 21 miles wide at its narrowest point.

For the Gulf, this is the whole game. Saudi Arabia is pushing more crude through its East-West pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea. The UAE is using the Habshan-Fujairah pipeline to bypass the strait entirely. Oman is selling itself as the safe alternative. The war has not closed the chokepoint, but, according to shipping officials, it has forced every Gulf producer to build a second route. That is expensive insurance that will outlast the conflict. The strait has stayed open, but the cost of keeping it open is now a permanent line item for every Gulf exporter.

Saudi Signs $1.16bn Battery Storage Deals
Saudi Arabia locked in four battery storage projects worth $1.16 billion, with a combined capacity of 2,000 megawatts. The Saudi Power Procurement Company signed the storage service agreements this week. The projects are the kind of unglamorous grid plumbing that determines whether a grid built for oil can handle solar.

The context is a power system that is adding renewable capacity faster than it can balance it. Battery storage is the cushion that lets solar and wind feed the grid without crashing it when the sun sets. Saudi Arabia is paying for that cushion now, before the grid gets bigger and the problem gets more expensive.

For contractors and suppliers, this is a signal that the kingdom's energy transition is moving from generation to stabilisation. The next wave of tenders will be for the systems that make the first wave work. The kingdom is now buying the grid infrastructure that makes its renewable targets physically possible.

UAE Fintech XSquare Raises Pre-Seed For Saudi Push
XSquare, a UAE-based fintech founded in 2023, raised an undisclosed pre-Seed round led by Raed Ventures, with participation from AngelSpark, 500 Global and Oraseya Capital. The startup is live in the UAE and Qatar, and the funding will pay for its Saudi launch. The rails are already in place: Telr, Geidea, VaultsPay, Spare and Tess Payments, plus a Mastercard partnership for B2B payments.

The name of the game is payment rail aggregation. XSquare does not build the pipes; it connects them. That is the unglamorous but essential work of making digital payments feel seamless across a region where every country has its own rules and its own incumbents.

For Gulf fintech watchers, a startup raising to enter Saudi Arabia is the standard plot. The kingdom is the region's biggest prize, and every UAE fintech with traction is now writing the same next chapter. The Saudi payment market is the last big unlock, and XSquare is one of several UAE fintechs racing to the border.

US-Iran Rhetoric Heats Up Ahead Of Sanctions
The US and Iran are exchanging defiant messages ahead of Monday's expected announcement of new American sanctions. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has promised the toughest measures ever against Iran. Tehran calls the sanctions economic terrorism and crimes against humanity. The war of words is real, but so is the market's ability to ignore it.

The sanctions could hit not just Iran but its most important trading partners, including China. That is the escalation the market has not fully priced in. If Washington goes after Chinese buyers of Iranian crude, the price response will not be modest.

For Gulf operators, the question is not whether the rhetoric matters but when it starts to move oil prices. The answer may already be here. Brent is trading near $94 a barrel, and each new announcement adds a risk premium that no one can hedge away. The next round of sanctions will test whether the oil market has already priced in the worst, or whether it has been hoping for the best.

Lisan Hits $2.6M ARR, Zero Churn
Lisan, an Arabic-first enterprise AI platform, grew to about $2.6 million in annual recurring revenue one year after winning the Rocket Fuel competition at LEAP 2025. The company maintains gross margins above 95 percent and zero churn across its government customers.

The numbers are the story. Zero churn in government means the product is sticky enough that no one leaves, which is rare in enterprise software. The margin is the more interesting detail. At 95 percent gross margin, Lisan is selling software, not services, which means it can scale without hiring an army of delivery staff.

Daleela, the women's health platform from the same ecosystem, has completed over 8,000 consultations and grown to 400,000 registered users, with more than a quarter in the GCC. That is the pattern: Arabic-first products are finding a market that global platforms have ignored. The Gulf is not just a buyer of AI. It is starting to build its own. The LEAP pipeline is producing companies that are not just surviving but building sticky, high-margin businesses on the region's own terms.

Gen Z Refuses the Penance Saving Model
Younger workers are saving, but not at the cost of travel, dining, and experiences. They call it soft saving, and the difference is emotional: safety, not sacrifice. That is a rational answer to a world where retirement feels abstract and rent feels immediate. Gulf banks that make savings feel like self-care instead of punishment win this wallet. The first Gulf financial brand to translate soft saving into Arabic product design will own a generation that distrusts old austerity messaging.

America's Data Centers Hit a Wall
Opposition to local data centers reached 75 percent in US polling this month. New York imposed the first state moratorium, and dozens of counties and towns banned them outright. The industry that sold itself on clean jobs now faces a land-use revolt driven by water, grid strain, and cooling demand. The Gulf sees a queue of hyperscalers looking for somewhere with cheap power and no veto. Saudi Arabia and the UAE are building exactly the friendly desert this backlash creates demand for, and they are billing it as the fastest yes in the market.

The Robot's Tip Jar
A Las Vegas bar is charging a mandatory 10 percent service fee for cocktails made by robotic arms. The money lands with human employees, but the question is who ends up with the tip once the machine is the most visible worker. Gulf hospitality is watching, because automation is arriving faster than most markets and the human touch is now the product. This is the pricing puzzle beneath the region's hospitality tech boom: what do you charge for a show, and who keeps it?
