The Lead
​
Saudi Arabia just sketched a rail line through Syria to Turkey, and the trucks have not even arrived yet.
Saudi Arabia signed four agreements with Syria this week to rebuild roads, railways, aviation, and postal services. Transport Minister Saleh Al-Jasser sealed the deals in Damascus, and the two sides now have six months to name the connection points. The road and rail framework runs five years and renews automatically.

The rail link is the prize. Riyadh plans a line through Jordan and Syria within three to four years that would carry goods, oil, gas, and passengers all the way to Turkey and Europe. Saudi Arabia has already committed SAR 7.5 billion to rebuild Aleppo airport, and STC is laying Syria's national fiber network. A digital infrastructure deal expected in Riyadh next week would connect submarine cables landing on the Syrian coast to networks in Syria, Jordan, and Saudi Arabia.

The money is already circling. Saudi and Syrian officials agreed to launch a joint Saudi-Syrian bank, and flynas is close to starting flynas Syria with Syrian Airlines before year-end. Saudi companies have also bid to run Syria's postal service.

For Gulf operators, Syria is no longer a headline about war. Ministers briefed on commercial terms this week are treating it as a construction site. The corridor is a direct answer to the Strait of Hormuz bottleneck, which, according to officials, has rerouted cargo since February. A trade route that bypasses the strait entirely changes the cost of doing business from Dammam to Duqm, and the rail contract is the first map of it. The companies that secure a stake in this corridor now will own the supply chain of the region's next decade.

02Grid storage
Saudi Arabia starts $1.16 billion battery build
Saudi Arabia picked builders for the first 8,000 MWh battery push, and the $1.16 billion projects will deliver four hours of storage. The projects will connect to the national grid under an agreement between Saudi Energy and the Expo 2030 Riyadh Company. The storage capacity is enough to keep a city running for hours after the sun goes down.

The kingdom is pairing solar and wind build-out with storage at a scale few markets have attempted. Battery projects of this size are the missing piece in the renewable expansion, because they let the grid use solar power at night instead of switching back to gas.

For Gulf operators, the storage build is a two-for-one: it locks in renewable contracts and creates demand for grid services, maintenance, and smart management software. Contractors and suppliers who get into the storage supply chain now will be selling into the kingdom's grid for the next decade, and the first group of projects is only the starting line. The companies that wire Saudi Arabia's batteries into the grid now will own the standard for every project that follows.

03War repair
EGA's smelter rebuild hits 25% completion
Emirates Global Aluminium's Al-Taweelah smelter restoration has reached 25% completion, according to the company, after Iranian drone and missile attacks damaged the facility in Abu Dhabi's Khalifa Economic Zone on March 28. The plant consists of 1,262 reduction cells, and the damage knocked out a significant share of the UAE's aluminium output. The rebuild is now a quarter done.

The repair work is a reminder that the war has concrete costs even for non-combatants. Aluminium prices have stayed firm because the Gulf is a major supplier, and any extended outage tightens the global market. For Gulf operators, the smelter rebuild is reportedly a live case study in industrial resilience. Supply chains for spare parts, skilled labor, and engineering services are all being tested at once. The companies that solve the restoration fast will be first in line for the next emergency, and the next one is already priced into every insurance contract in the region. Al-Taweelah's slow return to full output is a tax the war has already collected from Gulf industry.

04Rate hike
Gold slides 3.4% as Warsh hints at hike
Gold fell 3.4% last week to $4,504.1 an ounce, snapping a three-week winning streak, after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh reinforced expectations of higher interest rates. Silver led the decline with a 3.9% drop. Palladium bucked the trend, jumping 6.8% to a near three-month high.

The trigger was inflation: the US Personal Consumption Expenditures index rose 3.7% year-on-year in July, above the 3.6% expected. Traders now put the odds of a September rate hike at 58%, up from 36% before Warsh spoke. The two-year Treasury yield climbed 11 basis points to 4.34%.

For Gulf investors, higher US rates mean the dollar tightens and gold takes a step back. That is a headwind for regional miners, a relief for importers paying in dollars, and a signal that the inflation fight is not over. The rare bright spot was palladium, which Gulf investors track closely because of its role in catalytic converters and industrial demand. The coming Fed meeting is now the single biggest input into every Gulf portfolio for the next month. A rate hike in September would tighten global capital and give Gulf central banks an excuse to follow, so watch the Fed minutes, not just the Fed move.

05Oman road
Oman preps RO 13.5 million mountain road
Oman's Ministry of Transport, Communications and Information Technology announced that the Al Jabal Al Abyadh road project is in the preparatory phase. The project is valued at RO 13.5 million. The road will open access to one of the country's most rugged mountain areas.

The project is part of Oman's push to connect its interior for tourism and logistics. Roads like this one are not just transport links, they are economic corridors that turn scenic but hard-to-reach areas into developable land for hotels, farms, and residential projects. The preparatory phase means tendering is next, and contractors are already circling.

For Gulf construction firms, RO 13.5 million is a mid-sized job, but Oman's road program has a long tail. The companies that deliver this one on time and on budget will be first in line for the larger mountain infrastructure push, and the line is already forming. A mountain road that opens new land is not just an engineering job, it is an invitation to develop everything beyond it.

The AI Race
​
Music giants sue Anthropic over lyrics
Sony and Warner Music Group have filed a lawsuit against Anthropic, alleging a brazen campaign of intellectual property theft. The case targets how Anthropic's models ingest and reproduce copyrighted lyrics. It is one of the broadest claims yet filed against an AI lab over training data, and it marks a shift from negotiation to litigation across the music industry.

The labels argue that Anthropic's behavior goes far beyond fair use. If courts side with them, AI companies would reportedly be forced to strike licensing deals for the material they train on. Those fees would reshape the economics of generative AI, raising the cost of every song, show, and article that goes into a model. The case is being watched closely by every AI lab, because the damages number will set the market price for copyrighted text. For Gulf AI builders, this is the first invoice: budget for licensing now, because free training data is closing.
