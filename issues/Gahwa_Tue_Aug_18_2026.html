The Lead
Qatar's economy could shrink 12.4% this year. Oman is expected to grow.
​
BMI now expects the MENA economy to contract 3.3% in 2026, a downgrade from its earlier forecast of 0.9%.

The damage is wildly uneven across the Gulf. Qatar faces a 12.4% contraction, Kuwait 20.6%, and Iraq 19.4%. Saudi Arabia is expected to shrink 1.3%. Only two GCC economies stay positive: the UAE at 0.3% growth and Oman at 2.9%.

Moody's kept a stable outlook on Saudi Arabia's Aa3 rating. Fiscal buffers are estimated at 18% of GDP. The Kingdom can also reroute crude westward through the East-West Pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea, an option Qatar, Bahrain, and Kuwait do not have.

For Gulf operators, 2026 is now a year of defense. Governments that can shield spending will do so. Governments that cannot will cut projects, delay payments, and turn to debt markets. Kuwait and Qatar are already active in that direction. The UAE and Oman are the only places where a business can still plan for normal growth. The Gulf is splitting into two economies this year, and the line is whether a country can still spend.

Khorfakkan Port to double capacity
Khorfakkan Port aims to more than double annual handling capacity to 10 million containers, up from 5 million initially.

The Sharjah port sits outside the Strait of Hormuz. That has become a strategic prize at a moment when shipping through the strait has slowed after tanker attacks, according to shipping sources. The expansion would build a route that does not require a vessel to enter the Gulf.

For Gulf traders, the message is blunt. Redundancy is now a tariff on doing business. Ports, warehouses, and routes outside the strait are the new premium assets. Sharjah is moving first. A container terminal is now a geopolitical asset, and Sharjah just made a bid to become the region's outside-of-the-strait port.

Kuwait's $16 billion lifeline
KKR and other investors signed a $16 billion infrastructure deal with Kuwait Oil Company in July, the largest foreign direct investment in Kuwait's history.

Kuwait also received a $4.5 billion syndicated loan for its Investment Authority in early August. It issued a $6 billion bond in July that saw strong demand. Kuwait produced 1.97 million barrels of crude per day in July, up from 1.65 million in June.

The money is coming in, but the economy is still expected to contract 0.6% this year, according to the IMF. Kuwait's oil infrastructure faced Iranian attacks in July, reportedly causing injuries and significant material damage. The $16 billion deal is a vote of confidence and a repair bill at the same time.

For operators, the takeaway is that Kuwait has not lost access to capital, even as its economy shrinks. The question is whether it can spend it fast enough. Kuwait's problem was never access to money, according to officials; it is getting the money out of the ground and away from the missiles.

US-Iran war becomes political attrition
The US-Iran conflict has entered its fifth month and is now a phase of political attrition, according to a senior researcher at Trends Research and Advisory.

The US has partially achieved its military objectives. According to US officials, Iran's missile and drone capabilities are degraded, its air defense network weakened, and its naval threat reduced. But Iran is now signaling that it can endure pressure.

The regional cost is permanent. Countries relying on stable maritime routes and uninterrupted energy exports remain exposed to unpredictable escalation. Global markets are reacting to military developments and political messages from both sides.

For Gulf businesses, the attrition phase means no settlement and no decisive end. It means a long, slow squeeze on shipping costs, insurance premiums, and the willingness of foreign partners to sign long contracts. The war is no longer about a single escalation; it is about which side can keep paying the cost longer.
​
Mannai opens Riyadh headquarters
Mannai IT Saudi Arabia planted a Riyadh flag to anchor leadership, engineering, customer delivery, and local talent development.

The company has four business units: connected buildings and cities, enterprise software and platforms, cybertech and managed services, and cloud and digital workplace. It launched a Vision 2030 program called KSA Accelerate to develop Saudi-made technology products.

The move is part of a broader push by multinationals to anchor in Riyadh as Saudi Arabia enforces its regional headquarters rules. Mannai is betting that local presence, local hiring, and local product development will beat flying in salespeople from Dubai.

For the broader market, this is what Vision 2030 looks like from the inside: a race to hire Saudi engineers before the policy window closes. The Saudi market is no longer a sales territory; it is a hiring market, and the companies that treat it that way will win the next decade.

US childhood vaccine list cut from 18 to 11
President Trump cut the recommended childhood vaccine list from 18 to 11 by executive order. Vaccines for COVID-19, flu, rotavirus, hepatitis A and B, and meningococcal disease would fall under shared clinical decision-making. The American Academy of Pediatrics opposes the overhaul and insurers have committed to cover CDC-recommended vaccines through 2027. The Gulf has been a fast follower of US health standards, and now the benchmark itself is contested.

Stripe bets $7B on OpenRouter
Stripe has agreed to buy AI model-router OpenRouter for more than $7 billion, months after the startup raised money at a $1.3 billion valuation. OpenRouter gives developers access to more than 400 AI models through a single interface, acting as the tollbooth for the AI boom. Stripe already owns payments, and this deal adds the transaction rails for model access. Gulf investors have poured money into data centres, but the smarter bet may be the software layer that routes work to models, and that is where $7 billion just landed.

Gen Z launches businesses faster
New business applications filed by Gen Z Americans jumped 66% in June compared to 2025, according to Bank of America Institute. A Gusto study found 71% of Gen Z entrepreneurs used AI to launch their businesses last year. Lower-income households now account for 25% of all new businesses, up from under 20% in 2020. The barrier to starting a company is no longer money, but whether the system around a founder wants them to try.
