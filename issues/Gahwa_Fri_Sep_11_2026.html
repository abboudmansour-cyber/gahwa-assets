The Lead
​
Riyadh just launched the world's first robot sports league, and the athletes stand 1.73 meters tall with 29 joints.
The robots, built by EngineAI, carry 29 articulated joints and compete in teams managed by three roles: builders, coders, and drivers. The inaugural season will span eight cities across the Middle East, Europe, the Americas, and Asia, with two stops in the Gulf region.

Hero Esports, Asia's largest esports company, created the league and is establishing a global technology lab at its Shanghai headquarters to develop the underlying systems. HRH Prince Faisal bin Bandar bin Sultan al Saud, Chairman of the Saudi Esports Federation, commented on the event, signaling state-level backing for the concept. MENA creator AboFlah and Saudi sports creator Bashar Arabi served as competing team managers at the first event, held at Boulevard City.

Why robots instead of pixels The league is positioning itself as the physical layer of competitive entertainment, where engineering talent matters as much as reflexes. Each match is a test of the hardware, the software, and the driver's real-time decisions. That formula turns a stadium event into a showcase for the same skills Saudi Arabia is trying to import for its AI and manufacturing sectors.

The bet goes beyond entertainment Saudi Arabia is already the Gulf's most aggressive investor in esports and gaming infrastructure. CyberHero extends that play into the physical world, where the barriers to entry are higher and the potential for proprietary technology is greater. If the league scales, Riyadh becomes the reference point for a sport that did not exist a week ago. The first question is whether audiences will pay to watch machines compete, or whether the real product is the technology pipeline itself.

02Capital flows west
UAE pledges €40bn to Germany across AI and energy
The UAE committed €40 billion, or $46.5 billion, to Germany in a package spanning industry, AI, digital infrastructure, and energy. President Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed and Chancellor Friedrich Merz unveiled the pledge during a state visit. The commitment more than doubles the €34 billion Abu Dhabi has already invested in the German economy.

The deal includes a new German-UAE Investment Council and a Strategic Dialogue that reboots a partnership first signed in 2004. A set of 29 business-to-business agreements worth just over €9.36 billion involves Adnoc, Masdar, RWE, and Covestro. The two sides also committed to build 1 gigawatt of data center capacity in Germany, matching similar pledges the UAE has made with France and Italy.

Why the pattern matters The UAE has now signed data center and AI investment packages across Europe with Paris, Rome, Berlin, and Ankara, each carrying roughly 1 gigawatt of capacity. The France pledge has already yielded a 1.4-gigawatt AI campus near Paris with MGX backing. Germany is simply the latest canvas for the same playbook. For Gulf operators, this is the clearest signal yet that the UAE intends to be the region's bridge to European AI infrastructure.

03Hormuz under pressure
Hormuz transits fall as Houthis reportedly seize Mocha
Houthi forces reportedly seized the strategic Red Sea port city of Mocha and pushed toward the Hanish Islands near the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Saudi Arabia responded with dozens of airstrikes over a 24-hour period, according to officials. The offensive against Saudi-backed government forces has intensified the wider conflict.

Vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz fell to seven on Thursday, down from 11 the previous day and well below the 10-day average of 15. The figures exclude vessels with AIS transponders turned off. Brent crude settled above $100 per barrel for the first time since late May, with the latest trades near $108, according to market data. Hormuz is under pressure from the Iran war, and Bab el-Mandeb is threatened by the Houthi advance on the Red Sea coast. Saudi oil shipments through Yanbu on the Red Sea have recovered to about 3.7 million barrels per day, but that route now faces a renewed threat from the south. Shipping insurers and Gulf exporters are now looking at a scenario where both the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb are dangerous at the same time.

04MENA funding doubles
MENA startup funding jumps 117% in August
MENA startup funding reached $375 million across 27 deals in August 2026, more than doubling the prior month. Moove's $250 million Series C accounted for two-thirds of the total, an outsized anchor that flatters the headline number. The month also saw PayTabs strike a $100 million-plus deal for Amazon Payment Services MENA, according to reports, and Saudi FlyAkeed secured $25.15 million to scale its enterprise travel platform. Keep Converting closed a $2 million pre-seed led by Nuwa Capital and COTU Ventures. Sirdab raised $10 million in Series A funding to expand across the GCC.

Why the concentration matters One deal made two-thirds of the month's funding, and one country captured almost all of it. That concentration is both a strength and a warning sign. The ecosystem is deepening in the UAE, but the rest of the region is still waiting for its share of the capital. For founders outside the UAE, the data suggests the funding gap is widening, not narrowing, even as the headline numbers improve.

Retail's tourist hangover
Dubai retail shields itself as tourism drops
Dubai's retail sector is splitting in two, with tourist-heavy locations suffering sharp declines while local-facing businesses hold steady or grow. Sales at Mall of the Emirates fell almost 50% in March, according to GRMC Advisory Services. DXB passenger numbers dropped 31.3% year-on-year, and hotel occupancy fell 30.3% to 56.4% in the first half.

Tourist sales now account for just 5-10% of total retail revenue, down from 20-25% before the war, even though tourists still represent 15-20% of footfall. Maison Arabelle, a luxury abaya and kaftan atelier, saw footfall at its Dubai store rise 85% year-on-year in the second quarter and remain up 49% in the third. By contrast, Teeb Perfumes at Global Village saw customer flow and sales drop 10-30%, forcing temporary closures.

Why this matters for the wider economy Dubai's government has responded with relief programs worth AED 2.5 billion targeting tourism, trade, customs, and aviation. Online retail revenue has surged over 64% to $9.2 billion this year, according to Dubai South citing Statista. The question is whether the 160,000 new residents who arrived this year can offset the missing tourist spending. Fitch expects recovery no earlier than November, when non-regional airlines are likely to resume full flight schedules.

The AI Race
​
Intel assembles servers inside Saudi Arabia
Intel marked 25 years in Saudi Arabia by announcing a 'Saudi Made, Developed, Deployed' initiative with HPE and the Ministry of Communications. The program will produce locally assembled servers running Intel Xeon 6 processors. Lenovo announced its first Saudi-manufactured laptop at the same event, with plans to add desktops, workstations and servers.

The move lands at a moment when Gulf states are trying to attach sovereign AI capital to local industrial capacity. Intel also signed deals with stc group and Mobily for edge AI and video analytics, and it will train 100,000 people through a national digital readiness program by 2030. The message to Nvidia and AMD is clear: the Gulf wants factories, not just chips. Riyadh now hosts assembly lines from two of the world's largest computer makers, a step toward owning its AI hardware supply.

Sabic wins $3.47bn ammonia contract
Sabic Agri-Nutrients awarded a $3.47 billion EPC contract for its seventh facility in Jubail Industrial City. The contract went to Samsung E&A. The complex will produce ammonia and urea. The project strengthens Saudi Arabia's downstream chemicals position at a time when commodity prices reward scale.
KEC seals $184m Madinah project
Knowledge Economic City signed an agreement with Kaden Investment Company for Multaqa Al Madinah 2. The project spans 229,117 square meters with roughly 1,527 residential units. Total equity required is about $222 million.
Oman opens 500MW solar bid
Oman issued a request for proposals for a 500-megawatt solar plant in Sinaw, North Al-Sharqiyah Governorate. It is an independent power project. The tender adds to Oman's accelerating renewable energy pipeline.
Zeal raises $10m for checkout software
Egypt-based fintech Zeal raised $10 million, bringing its total to $14 million. The startup builds software that works with existing payment terminals to identify and reward customers. It has contracts to activate on more than four million card machines over 24 months.

Off Paper
​
A football club that teaches
Benfica launched an institute to sell its training and management expertise, and the first course is already underway. It exports the knowledge that cost millions to develop internally. Gulf clubs have spent heavily on foreign talent; this is the playbook for selling their own academies back.
Marrakech's coffee grows up
Five specialty coffee shops now anchor a scene that is barely five years old. One sources matcha from Japan, another serves beans from Amsterdam and Australia. The same roaster-led boom that reportedly hit Riyadh and Dubai is now climbing the Atlas Mountains.
The contrarian optician
Ahmed Ragab sold 51% of Baraka Retail Group and stayed on, which is rare enough. He also says AI should not be forced where it does not belong. A second-generation family businessman who kept his job and his skepticism.

Oman's mountain rise
Oman's Al Jabal Al Akhdhar has been named GCC Tourism Capital for 2027. The mountain region is known for terraced farms, a cool climate and dramatic landscapes. The title is a marketing platform as much as an honor. Oman is finally getting a turn as the Gulf's quiet alternative to the glitzy hubs.
The Last Sip
​
Which Gulf country has more oil and fewer people than almost anywhere else?
The answer
Kuwait. It sits on roughly 6% of the world's proven oil reserves with a population of about 4.3 million. That works out to around 23,000 barrels of proven oil per resident. Only a handful of countries are richer in oil per person. The housing is expensive, but at least the national holdings are well diversified underground.
