The Lead
​
Riyadh Expo Bound
The US signed its participation contract for Expo 2030 Riyadh on Saturday, and the ink went to paper in Atlanta, not Riyadh.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Saudi Foreign Minister Faisal bin Farhan signed the agreement on the sidelines of the G20 foreign ministers' meeting. The US Pavilion will occupy a spot at the six-month exhibition that opens October 2030.

The signing carries a clear message: Washington wants the world to see that bilateral business with Riyadh continues even as the regional security map shifts. Reportedly, Expo 2030 is the single largest international event ever hosted in the Kingdom, and the US presence anchors its credibility with other delegations.

For Gulf operators, this locks in a seven-year runway of Expo-related construction, services, and hospitality demand that no other event in the region can match. The US commitment turns Expo 2030 from a Saudi ambition into a fixed point on the global calendar. The spending that follows will touch every sector from construction to hospitality.

02Oil still in the hole
Saudi Non-Oil Grows Despite 3.8% GDP Drop
Saudi Arabia's economy contracted 3.8% year-on-year in the third quarter as oil activity dropped 20.4%.

The non-oil private sector still grew 1.9% over the same period, per Gastat. The drag comes from the war: eight months of US-Iran fighting has restricted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, hitting production and exports, reportedly. The IMF still expects the Kingdom to contract 3.3% this year before rebounding to 7.9% growth in 2027.

Mood check S&P Global affirmed the economy at A+ with a stable outlook, forecasting a 0.9% contraction this year. Reportedly, for Gulf operators, the number confirms that non-oil sectors remain the only reliable growth engine while the strait stays shut. Riyadh is running two economies at once: an oil sector in wartime decline and a non-oil base still pulling forward. This means the Vision 2030 bet is holding while the barrels wait for Hormuz.

04Ads for AI Agents
Publishers Sell Ads Only AI Agents See
Publishers are now serving ads that only AI agents can see, and TIME is already doing it.

Using a system from Mobian, the publisher delivers machine-readable pages with labeled sponsored blocks to registered AI agents. European publishers recorded 227 AI scrapes per human visitor referred by AI in Q2 2026, up from 150 the prior quarter.

More than 100,000 advertisers have already run ChatGPT ads, and OpenAI is testing 'Sponsored Agents' that open a labeled chat with a business on click. Meanwhile, Anthropic is keeping Claude ad-free.

The media economy is splitting in two: a human-facing ad market and a machine-facing one, each with its own pricing and its own honesty problem. For Gulf publishers and brands, the question is whether your content is readable enough for an AI agent to buy from. The first ad no human ever sees has already been sold, and the unit of measurement is no longer the click but the scrape.
Source: The Neuron

05Unlisted numbers
Oracle Expands Secret Saudi Data Centers
Oracle is expanding its data center network in Saudi Arabia and keeping the site locations secret, citing security.

The company already operates cloud infrastructure regions in Jeddah and Riyadh, and the Middle East is the fastest-growing part of its EMEA business.

This is the less glamorous side of the AI boom: the physical boxes that make the models run. Oracle is quietly becoming the landlord while everyone else argues over the tenants. For Gulf operators, the signal is clear: hyperscale capacity is arriving faster than public maps show.

The AI Race
​
AI Hits a Data Wall
Nearly two-thirds of GCC organizations use AI for specific business functions, but just 4% have embedded it companywide.

The wall is data. Only 34% of firms in a Cloudera survey have fully integrated data sources across their environments. Mashreq's Chief AI Officer Xi Liang says legacy systems refresh only every 10 minutes. That is too slow for real-time conversational AI. AIQ, the Adnoc-Presight venture, sends engineers to spend an average of 100 days on-site mapping and consolidating a customer's data before training any model. Seventy-two hours of engineers on-site per model, because ten-minute legacy refreshes cannot power a conversation.
